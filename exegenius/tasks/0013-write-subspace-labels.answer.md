A subspace label is a nondegenerate subspace of a fixed bilinear space, attached to each gate and terminal of a network. Labels fix how much work each change of representation costs, and the subset-intersection network and the complex phase network that follow are built from them.

Let $E$ denote the fixed bilinear space, with bilinear form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, and let its dimension be $m$, the number of coordinates of a vector of $E$. Assume $E$ is nondegenerate. A label is a nondegenerate subspace $U$ of $E$. A subspace $W$ is nondegenerate when no nonzero vector of $W$ is orthogonal to every vector of $W$. The orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ is the set of vectors of $E$ orthogonal to every vector of $W$.

The construction attaches <cite data-source="paper">subspaces of a fixed m-dimensional space</cite> to its gates. The labels are chosen <cite data-source="paper">to make the changes of frame inexpensive</cite>, so the page's question is which subspaces make a change of frame cheap.

Subspace labels are built on [linear networks and frames](linear-network-frames.md). A vertex whose label is $U$ has frame $C_U$, the operator that turns the logical array into the stored array there. In the complex network, each label $U$ gives a frame $C_U$ whose entries lie in [Gaussian dyadics](gaussian-dyadics.md). Each entry of $C_U$ and its inverse is a Gaussian integer divided by $2^m$ <cite data-source="paper">Gaussian integer divided by 2m</cite>.

On an edge, $U$ and $V$ are its tail and head labels. <cite data-source="paper">On every edge one label will contain the other</cite>. Say $V \subseteq U$. The orthogonal residual of the edge is $U\cap V^\perp$, <cite data-source="paper">the orthogonal complement of the smaller label inside the larger one</cite>. Since $V$ is nondegenerate, $E = V \oplus V^\perp$, and intersecting with $U \supseteq V$ gives $U = V \oplus (U\cap V^\perp)$. So the residual has dimension $\dim U - \dim V$, the absolute change in label dimension. That dimension counts <cite data-source="paper">the smaller operations needed on an edge</cite>. <cite data-source="paper">The saving will come from keeping the total dimension lost on decreasing</cite> edges small.

Across the edge from $U$ to $V$, the frame changes by $C_V C_U^{-1}$. The source writes this change as a product of translation kernels: <cite data-source="paper">is a product of one translation kernel aI + bXv , or its inverse, per</cite> vector of an orthonormal basis of the residual. Here $(X_v f)(x) = f(x+v)$ <cite data-source="paper">(Xv f )(x) = f (x + v)</cite>.

Let $h$ be the size of a ground set, and let $J$ be the all-one $h\times h$ matrix. For the bit construction, <cite data-source="paper">For the bit network, let F = Qh with bilinear form</cite> $\langle x,y\rangle = x^{\top}(I-J/9)y$ on $\mathbb{Q}^h$. A triple $T$ is a three-element subset of the ground set, with indicator vector $t_T\in\mathbb{Q}^h$. Then $\langle t_T,t_T\rangle = 3 - 9/9 = 2$, so the line spanned by $t_T$ is nondegenerate. For another triple $S$, $\langle t_S,t_T\rangle = |S\cap T| - 1$, so triples sharing exactly one element give orthogonal lines. <cite data-source="paper">Thus every triple line is nondegenerate, and neighboring triple lines are orthogonal, in the label</cite> field.
