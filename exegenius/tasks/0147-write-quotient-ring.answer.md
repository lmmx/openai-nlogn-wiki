The quotient ring of a polynomial ring by several relations is the ring obtained by identifying two polynomials whenever their difference is a combination of the relations, so that every relation becomes zero. A later step uses this ring to place one list of coefficients on several cyclic axes at once.

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the ring of complex polynomials in the formal variables $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_M$, where $M$ is the number of variables. Let $f_1,\dots,f_K$ be polynomials in $\mathcal{P}$, the relations, where $K$ is the number of relations.

The ideal generated by the relations, $J$, is the set of all sums of the relations multiplied by arbitrary polynomials $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_K$:

$$J=\{\lambda_1 f_1+\dots+\lambda_K f_K \;:\; \lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_K\in\mathcal{P}\}.$$

An ideal is a set closed under addition and under multiplication by any polynomial. $J$ is one, since multiplying a combination of the relations by a polynomial gives another such combination.

Two polynomials $\phi,\psi$ are identified when $\phi-\psi\in J$, and the class of $\phi$ is written $[\phi]$. The classes form the quotient ring $\mathcal{P}/J$, with $[\phi]+[\psi]=[\phi+\psi]$ and $[\phi][\psi]=[\phi\psi]$. These operations do not depend on the chosen representatives: if $\phi-\phi'$ and $\psi-\psi'$ lie in $J$, then

$$\phi\psi-\phi'\psi'=\phi(\psi-\psi')+(\phi-\phi')\psi'$$

also lies in $J$.

Every relation is zero in the quotient, $[f_i]=0$, since $f_i\in J$. For example, take $M=2$, $K=2$, $f_1=\theta_1^2-1$ and $f_2=\theta_2^2+1$. Then $\theta_1^2-1=f_1\in J$ gives $[\theta_1^2]=[1]$, and likewise $[\theta_2^2]=[-1]$, so

$$[\theta_1^3\theta_2^2]=[\theta_1]\,[\theta_1^2]\,[\theta_2^2]=-[\theta_1].$$

With one variable and the single relation $y^r+1$, the quotient ring is reduced to the [Polynomial ring modulo yʳ + 1](polynomial-ring.md), whose elements are the polynomials of degree less than $r$. This holds because $y^r+1$ is monic, so every class contains exactly one polynomial of that degree.
