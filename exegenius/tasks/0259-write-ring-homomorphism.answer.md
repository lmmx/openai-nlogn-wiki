A ring homomorphism is a structure-preserving map between rings, and an isomorphism is one that is also bijective. The paper uses such a map to carry a convolution into a polynomial ring, where it can be computed.

A ring homomorphism is a map $\theta$ from one ring to another that preserves both operations. Here $\xi$ and $\eta$ denote arbitrary elements of the domain, and $\theta(\xi+\eta)=\theta(\xi)+\theta(\eta)$, $\theta(\xi\eta)=\theta(\xi)\theta(\eta)$, and $\theta(1)=1$. It is an isomorphism when it is also bijective, meaning one-to-one and onto. Then its inverse also preserves both operations, so a product computed in the target maps back to a product in the domain.

**Checking a map out of a quotient ring.** Let $Q=\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^2-1)$ be the ring of complex polynomials in an indeterminate $t$, where the relation $t^2-1=0$ is imposed: any multiple of $t^2-1$ counts as zero. Every element of $Q$ has the form $\alpha+\delta t$ for some complex numbers $\alpha$ and $\delta$, which are its coefficients; they are unique because no nonzero polynomial of degree below 2 is a multiple of $t^2-1$. A map $\theta$ out of $Q$ that fixes complex scalars, so $\theta(\alpha)=\alpha$, is determined by $\theta(t)$, since $\theta(\alpha+\delta t)=\alpha+\delta\,\theta(t)$. It is well defined only if $\theta(t)^2=1$, because $t^2-1=0$ must map to $0$. Conversely, if $s$ in the target satisfies $s^2=1$, then $\alpha+\delta t\mapsto\alpha+\delta s$ is a ring homomorphism: it is substitution $t\mapsto s$, which preserves sums and products, and it sends $t^2-1$ to $0$.

The target $Q'=\mathbb{C}[y]/(y^2+1)$ is the ring of complex polynomials in an indeterminate $y$ with $y^2=-1$, the same kind of ring as $Q$; every element of $Q'$ has a unique form $u+vy$ with $u,v\in\mathbb{C}$.

**Example.** Define $\theta: Q\to Q'$ by $\theta(\alpha+\delta t)=\alpha+\delta\sqrt{-1}\,y$, so $\theta(t)=\sqrt{-1}\,y$. In $Q'$, $(\sqrt{-1}\,y)^2=-y^2=1$, so $\theta$ is well defined. Products are preserved: in $Q$, $(1+t)^2=2+2t$, while in $Q'$
$$(1+\sqrt{-1}\,y)^2=1+2\sqrt{-1}\,y+(\sqrt{-1}\,y)^2=2+2\sqrt{-1}\,y=\theta(2+2t).$$
Every $u+vy$ is the image of exactly one element of $Q$: $\theta(\alpha+\delta t)=u+vy$ exactly when $\alpha=u$ and $\delta=-\sqrt{-1}\,v$. So $\theta$ is bijective, with inverse sending $1+y$ to $1-\sqrt{-1}\,t$, since $\theta(1-\sqrt{-1}\,t)=1+y$. Hence $\theta$ is an isomorphism.

**A map that is not an isomorphism.** Let $\rho: Q\to\mathbb{C}$ be $\rho(\alpha+\delta t)=\alpha+\delta$. It is a ring homomorphism, since $\rho(t)=1$ and $1^2=1$. It is not bijective: $t-1$ is nonzero in $Q$, because it is not a multiple of $t^2-1$, yet $\rho(t-1)=0$. So $Q$ and $\mathbb{C}$ do not have the same structure.

**Why the paper needs it.** The paper uses this same check. Its map $\Phi$ goes from a cyclic quotient ring, polynomials in one indeterminate reduced by one relation, to a multivariate quotient ring, polynomials in several indeterminates with one relation for each. The check is <cite data-source="paper">respects xS = 1, so it defines an algebra map.</cite> Here the paper's letter $x$ is the generator of its cyclic ring, and $S$ is a positive integer. The map sends the generator to a monomial whose powers cover the target basis, so $\Phi$ is bijective as well as a homomorphism: <cite data-source="paper">through all target basis monomials exactly once.</cite> The paper's conclusion is <cite data-source="paper">Hence Φ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic</cite> convolution. An isomorphism of this kind preserves cyclic convolution: the convolution of two sequences in one ring maps to the convolution of their images in the other.
