Bluestein's chirp identity rewrites the [Discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) $F_q$ of even length $q$ as a convolution multiplied by chirp factors. In the argument it turns the [middle transform](middle-transform.md), including its target permutation, into a [normalized cyclic convolution](normalized-cyclic-convolution.md), which the paper's convolution method can compute.

The middle transform is the factor QFt in the paper's equation (35), the Fourier transform with the paper's target permutation $Q$ applied to its outputs. The paper states this factor as <cite data-source="paper">The factor QFt in (35) can be computed from a cyclic convolution and two pointwise phase</cite> multiplications. The reason to rewrite it as a convolution is that a convolution can be computed by any method for convolution, so the transform inherits that method's cost. The paper's method is given in its later sections: <cite data-source="paper">Sections 6 and 7 compute convolution on the remaining axes over this polynomial ring.</cite>

The exponential $\exp(x)$ is the exponential function, written $e^x$ elsewhere. For real $\theta$, Euler's formula $\exp(i\theta)=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ defines the exponential of an imaginary number. It gives $|\exp(i\theta)|^2=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$, and $\exp(2\pi i\mu)=1$ for every integer $\mu$, because cosine and sine repeat every $2\pi$.

The transform is $(F_q u)_\ell=\frac1q\sum_{s=0}^{q-1}u_s\,\omega^{s\ell}$, where $\omega=\exp(-2\pi i/q)$, so $\omega^{s\ell}=\exp(-2\pi i s\ell/q)$. Here $s$ is an input index and $\ell$ an output index. Let $\sigma$ be an integer position, which may be negative or at least $q$. The chirp is the vector $\chi$ whose entry at $\sigma$ is $\chi_\sigma=\exp(\pi i\sigma^2/q)$. Each chirp entry has modulus $1$, because $\pi\sigma^2/q$ is real.

Since $(\sigma+q)^2-\sigma^2=q(2\sigma+q)$, we get $\chi_{\sigma+q}=\chi_\sigma\exp(\pi i(2\sigma+q))$. Because $q$ is even, $2\sigma+q=2\mu$ with the integer $\mu=\sigma+q/2$, so the factor is $\exp(2\pi i\mu)=1$. Hence $\chi_{\sigma+q}=\chi_\sigma$, and $\chi_\sigma$ depends only on $\sigma$ modulo $q$. For odd $q$ the factor is $-1$ and each wrap-around would flip a sign.

For integers $s$ and $\ell$, the identity $2s\ell=s^2+\ell^2-(\ell-s)^2$ gives $\exp(-2\pi i s\ell/q)=\chi_{\ell-s}/(\chi_s\chi_\ell)$. Define $\hat u$ by $\hat u_s=u_s/\chi_s$. Writing $a\cdot b$ for the coordinatewise product, $(a\cdot b)_s=a_sb_s$, this is $\hat u=\frac1\chi\cdot u$. Substituting into the transform gives
$$(F_q u)_\ell=\frac{1}{\chi_\ell}\cdot\frac1q\sum_{s=0}^{q-1}\hat u_s\,\chi_{\ell-s}.$$
For length-$q$ sequences, $a\ast b$ denotes the cyclic convolution with $(a\ast b)_\ell=\sum_{s=0}^{q-1}a_s\,b_{(\ell-s)\bmod q}$. By periodicity $\chi_{\ell-s}=\chi_{(\ell-s)\bmod q}$, so the sum is $\hat u\ast\chi$ and the normalized convolution $\hat u\circledast\chi$ is $\frac1q(\hat u\ast\chi)$. Therefore
$$F_q u=\frac1\chi\cdot(\hat u\circledast\chi).$$
Both chirp multiplications change only phases, since they multiply by entries of modulus $1$. The only convolution is the middle one. It reduces the transform to a [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md), so any method that computes that convolution also computes the transform.

The wrap-around is what makes the sum cyclic. For $s>\ell$ the index $\ell-s$ is negative, and periodicity replaces $\chi_{\ell-s}$ by $\chi_{(\ell-s)\bmod q}$. For even $q$ the wrap-around carries no sign, so the identity avoids a [negacyclic convolution](negacyclic-convolution.md), a convolution in which a wrap-around picks up a minus sign.

The identity is applied inside the [transform identity with retained permutations](transform-identity.md), which keeps the permutations in the formula. Here output $\ell$ is written at position $\ell$ by its own factor $1/\chi_\ell$, so no sort of the output follows the convolution. The paper states that <cite data-source="paper">The chirp identity below performs the frequency change in Q without applying</cite> Q as a separate permutation of the array.

Worked example, with $q=2$. Here $F_2$ means $F_q$ at $q=2$, the transform of length $2$, not the binary field. Then $\omega=-1$, $\chi_0=1$ and $\chi_1=\exp(\pi i/2)=i$. Take $u=(1,3)$. Directly, $(F_2u)_0=(1+3)/2=2$ and $(F_2u)_1=(1-3)/2=-1$. By the identity, $\hat u=(1,-3i)$. Then $(\hat u\ast\chi)_0=1\cdot1+(-3i)\cdot i=4$ and $(\hat u\ast\chi)_1=1\cdot i+(-3i)\cdot1=-2i$, so $\hat u\circledast\chi=(2,-i)$. Multiplying by $1/\chi=(1,-i)$ gives $(2,-1)$, the same result.

The two chirp multiplications cost $O(q)$ steps. If the convolution takes $T(q)$ steps, the transform takes $T(q)+O(q)$ steps. The identity gives its cost with the [logarithm](logarithm.md) $\lg q$, the exponent to which $2$ must be raised to give $q$: if the convolution costs $O(q\lg q)$ steps, so does the transform.
