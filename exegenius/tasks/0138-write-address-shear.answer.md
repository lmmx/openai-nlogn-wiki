An address shear is the map that adds one address field to another and leaves the second unchanged. Interchanging address chunks on tape reduces to shears, whose cost enters the main bound.

Here $q$ is a fixed odd prime. Here $m$ is the number of fields in each vector. The address fields $H$ and $D$ are vectors of $m$ fields, $H_1, \ldots, H_m$ and $D_1, \ldots, D_m$. Each field is a block of $b$ radix-$q$ digits (digits in base $q$), <cite data-source="paper">every field ranging over [q b ]</cite>, so its values form the residue ring $\mathbb{Z}/q^b\mathbb{Z}$.

On a pair of address fields, the shear maps $(H, D)$ to $(H + D, D)$. More generally, a shear is given by a [matrix](linear-algebra-basics.md) $M$, a fixed rational $m \times m$ array: the shear with matrix $M$ sends $(H, D)$ to $(H + MD, D)$. The shear with matrix $M$ is counted by its [rational rank](matrix-rank.md) $a$, the number of independent rows over the rational numbers. The shear is computed with denominators [invertible modulo $q$](modular-inverse.md). <cite data-source="paper">Choose an odd prime avoiding every denominator</cite>.

The shear with matrix $M$ <cite data-source="paper">can be performed with exactly a interchanges of pairs of b-digit fields and O(V ) further work</cite>. Each interchange costs $O(V u^{\tau})$ by the bound on [Address-field interchange](address-interchange.md), where $u$ is the field width in bits. So an address shear of rank $a$ is implemented by $a$ uses of one [Address-field interchange](address-interchange.md). For fixed $q$, $u$ is a constant multiple of $b$, so the total cost is $O(aVb^{\tau}) + O(V)$.

Why $a$ interchanges suffice: <cite data-source="paper">where E1 , E2 are invertible lower triangular rational matrices and Π has exactly a entries equal to</cite> one. Hence $M = E_1\Pi E_2$, with $E_1$ and $E_2$ invertible lower-triangular and $\Pi$ a $0/1$ matrix with exactly $a$ ones. The procedure is <cite data-source="paper">first transform D by E2 and H by E1−1 , then add ΠD to H, and finally</cite> transform $H$ by $E_1$ and $D$ by $E_2^{-1}$.

<cite data-source="paper">Each one in position (i, j) of Π calls for Hi ← Hi + Dj</cite>, where $i$ and $j$ are the row and column of that one. That update is one interchange: $D_j \leftarrow D_j + H_i$, swap $H_i$ and $D_j$, then $D_j \leftarrow H_i - D_j$. <cite data-source="paper">A lower triangular transformation of H, or of D, costs O(V )</cite>, so the total is $a$ interchanges plus $O(V)$ work.

When $m = 1$, write $h$ and $d$ for the current values of $H$ and $D$. On an initial pair $(h, d)$, <cite data-source="paper">this sequence yields (h + d, d).</cite> For example, with $q = 3$, $b = 2$ (residues modulo $9$), $h = 3$ and $d = 5$: $D \leftarrow D + H$ gives $8$, the swap gives $H = 8$ and $D = 3$, and $D \leftarrow H - D$ gives $5$. So $(3, 5)$ becomes $(8, 5)$.

The shear is computed by the [Linear networks and frames](linear-network-frames.md) of the [bit procedure](bit-procedure.md). On every role, this shear is the [endpoint operator](endpoint-operator.md) of the bit procedure <cite data-source="paper">For the bit procedure, that endpoint operator is an address shear</cite>. On each role, this shear is realised as the endpoint operator of the network.

Shears are counted by rank because each costs its rank in interchanges. <cite data-source="paper">The number of recursive calls is the sum of the edge ranks, exactly s.</cite> Each call acts on one <cite data-source="paper">stream of volume V /W</cite>, with $W$ the number of wires. <cite data-source="paper">Because the sum of these ranks is less than the number of wires</cite> times $m$, the comparison is with $Wm$, and <cite data-source="paper">the resulting recurrence saves a power of the chunk width.</cite> Saving means the cost falls below the trivial bound by a power of the chunk width.
