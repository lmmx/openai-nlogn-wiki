Cyclic convolution is the product of two sequences of equal length in which an index that runs past the end wraps around to the start. The paper's algorithm for [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md) needs it because its coefficient layout and middle transform both become cyclic convolutions.

For sequences $f = (f_0, \dots, f_{n-1})$ and $g = (g_0, \dots, g_{n-1})$, indices are read modulo $n$, so $g_{-1}$ means $g_{n-1}$. The cyclic convolution $f \ast g$ has entries

$$(f \ast g)_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} f_j \, g_{(k-j) \bmod n}, \qquad 0 \le k < n,$$

where $k$ is the output position and $j$ is the position in $f$ of each summand. An index that reaches $n$ wraps back to the start. [Ordinary (linear) convolution](convolution.md), by contrast, has output length $2n-1$.

Identify $f$ with $f(w) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} f_j w^j$, where $w$ is a formal variable of this page. Let $\varepsilon$ be the offset of an exponent past $n$, with $0 \le \varepsilon \le n-2$. Then $f \ast g$ is the product in the [polynomial ring](polynomial-ring.md) $\mathbb{C}[w]/(w^n - 1)$: form $f(w)g(w)$, replace each power $w^{n+\varepsilon}$ by $w^\varepsilon$, and read off the coefficients. This works because $w^n = 1$ in the quotient.

Take $n = 4$, $f = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ and $g = (1, 1, 0, 0)$. Their ordinary product is $1 + 3w + 5w^2 + 7w^3 + 4w^4$. Replacing $w^4$ by $1$ gives $f \ast g = (5, 3, 5, 7)$.

The first step is the coefficient layout, which the paper motivates as: <cite data-source="paper">we first place the ordinary coefficient stream in its tensor order</cite>. Its model is Agarwal and Cooley's split. A cyclic convolution of length $n$ is split by the [Chinese remainder theorem](chinese-remainder-theorem.md) when $n = n_1 \cdots n_d$ with pairwise coprime $n_1, \dots, n_d$ (no common factor above 1), giving a $d$-dimensional cyclic convolution with axis $\kappa$, $1 \le \kappa \le d$, of length $n_\kappa$. The paper notes that <cite data-source="paper">Cooley used the Chinese remainder theorem to convert one cyclic convolution into multidimensional</cite> cyclic convolution.

Pick integers $\mu_\kappa$, each invertible modulo $n_\kappa$, and let $\Phi$ send $w$ to $\prod_\kappa \nu_\kappa^{\mu_\kappa}$, where $\nu_\kappa$ is the variable of axis $\kappa$, in $\mathbb{C}[\nu_1,\dots,\nu_d]/(\nu_\kappa^{n_\kappa}-1)$. This is well defined because $(\prod_\kappa \nu_\kappa^{\mu_\kappa})^n = 1$. Then $\Phi(w^k)$ puts the coefficient at index $k$ at the array position with coordinates $\mu_\kappa k \bmod n_\kappa$. These positions are distinct for $0 \le k < n$ by the Chinese remainder theorem. The paper concludes: <cite data-source="paper">Hence Φ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic</cite> convolution. A ring isomorphism sends products to products, so $\Phi(f \ast g) = \Phi(f)\Phi(g)$, a product on the array, which is a cyclic convolution on each axis.

The second step is the middle transform, the factor $QFt$ in the paper's equation (35), a Fourier-type transform. The paper states that <cite data-source="paper">The factor QFt in (35) can be computed from a cyclic convolution</cite>. Let $t$ be even, $\omega = e^{2\pi i/t}$, and let $F_t u$ have entries $\sum_j \omega^{jk} u_j$. From $2jk = j^2 + k^2 - (k-j)^2$ we get $\omega^{jk} = \chi_j \chi_k \tau(k-j)$, where $\chi_j = e^{\pi i j^2/t}$ and $\tau(\delta) = e^{-\pi i \delta^2/t}$. Since $t$ is even, $\tau(\delta + t) = \tau(\delta)$, so $\tau$ is a sequence of length $t$ read modulo $t$. With $\cdot$ the coordinatewise product, $F_t u = \chi \cdot \big((\chi \cdot u) \ast \tau\big)$: two pointwise phase multiplications around one cyclic convolution.
