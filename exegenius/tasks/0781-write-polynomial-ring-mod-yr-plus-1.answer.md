Here $y$ is the polynomial variable, an indeterminate (a symbol with no numeric value) whose powers act as roots of unity. Here $r$ is the record length, the number of complex coefficients in a polynomial record, which is a power of two. Earlier pages use $y$ for an argument or an input; on this page it has only the meaning given here.

The polynomial ring modulo $y^r+1$ is the set of complex polynomials in which $y^r$ is identified with $-1$, so that a power of $y$ that runs past the top position wraps around with its sign reversed. It is the storage for one coordinate of the array in the [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md) argument: after a further conversion to convolution, that coordinate is stored <cite data-source="paper">as the coefficient list of a polynomial modulo y r + 1, with r a power of two</cite>.

Write $R_r=\mathbb{C}[y]/(y^r+1)$ for the set of classes of complex polynomials, where two polynomials lie in the same class when they differ by a multiple of $y^r+1$. Concretely, two polynomials are in one class when their difference is $(y^r+1)\,q(y)$ for a polynomial $q(y)$ with complex coefficients, the quotient. The degree of a polynomial is the largest exponent with a nonzero coefficient, and its leading coefficient is the coefficient at that exponent. Each class contains a polynomial of degree less than $r$, called its representative. Below, $e$ is an integer exponent of $y$. To find a representative, replace each power $y^e$ with $e\ge r$ by $-y^{e-r}$. This is valid because $y^e=y^{e-r}\,y^r=-y^{e-r}$, and repeating the replacement ends at degree less than $r$.

Each class has exactly one representative, written $\sum_{d=0}^{r-1}c_d\,y^d$, where $d$ is the position of a coefficient, running from $0$ to $r-1$, and $c_d$ is the complex coefficient at position $d$. The list $(c_0,\dots,c_{r-1})$ is the coefficient list of the class. <cite data-source="paper">We always use representatives of degree less than r.</cite> Uniqueness: two representatives of one class differ by $(y^r+1)\,q(y)$ and have degree below $r$. If $q\neq 0$, the product has degree $\deg q+r\ge r$, because $y^r+1$ has leading coefficient $1$. So $q=0$, and the two representatives are equal.

Addition of representatives is coefficientwise and keeps degree below $r$. For multiplication, multiply two representatives as ordinary polynomials and reduce the product as above. The result does not depend on the representatives chosen: changing one factor by $(y^r+1)\,q(y)$ changes the product by a multiple of $y^r+1$. Multiples of $y^r+1$ are closed under addition and under multiplication by any polynomial, so the ring axioms that $\mathbb{C}[y]$ satisfies carry over to these classes. Hence $R_r$ satisfies the axioms of [Ring](ring.md).

A root of unity is an element some positive power of which equals $1$; the least such power is its order. Since $y^r=-1$, we have $y^{2r}=(-1)^2=1$. For $0<e<r$, $y^e$ has degree $e\ge 1$ while $1$ has degree $0$, so $y^e\neq 1$. For $r\le e<2r$, $y^e=-y^{e-r}$ equals $-1$ when $e=r$, and otherwise has degree at least $1$; either way it is not $1$. So $y$ is a root of unity of order $2r$.

Take $r=4$, so $y^4=-1$, and consider the element $1+2y$, with coefficient list $(1,2,0,0)$. Multiplying by $y^3$ gives $y^3+2y^4=y^3-2$, with coefficient list $(-2,0,0,1)$. The coefficient $1$ at position $0$ moved to position $3$. The coefficient $2$ at position $1$ would move to position $4$, which is position $0$ with its sign reversed, giving $-2$ there.

Let $e$ satisfy $0\le e<r$. Multiplying $\sum_d c_d y^d$ by $y^e$ gives $\sum_d c_d y^{d+e}$. If $d+e<r$, the coefficient $c_d$ moves unchanged to position $d+e$. If $d+e\ge r$, then $y^{d+e}=-y^{d+e-r}$, so $c_d$ moves to position $d+e-r$ with its sign reversed. Multiplication by $y^e$ therefore permutes the coefficient list and <cite data-source="paper">changes some signs</cite>. The wrap-around is negacyclic: it reverses the sign of a coefficient that passes the top position, whereas the wrap in a [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md) keeps it.

This is why the ring helps. A fast transform of length $N$ runs in $\log_2 N$ levels, and each level multiplies entries by roots of unity. With a general complex root, each such multiplication is a complex multiplication. Here the roots are powers of $y$, which serve <cite data-source="paper">as roots of unity in the remaining axes, and multiplication by such a root is a signed shift of the</cite> coefficient list. A signed shift does no complex arithmetic: each coefficient is copied to a new position, and a wrap changes only the sign carried with the copied value. So multiplying by one of these roots costs only data movement.

This ring gives the arithmetic for [Synthetic polynomial transforms](synthetic-transforms.md), whose roots of unity are powers of $y$.
