Scratch storage is auxiliary storage that a procedure may overwrite during its work but must restore to its starting contents, whatever those contents were. The subset-intersection network uses its scratch wires as the side and central wires that carry intermediate sums, so that the network can exchange two banks of values without leaving any trace in those wires. Later pages build on this restoration: the address-field interchange and the packed selected-bit addition both run such networks on arrays.

The point of restoring the scratch wires is that the network must not assume anything about their starting values. A tape machine reuses its work space, so a scratch wire may still hold values left by earlier computation. The paper therefore states the requirement directly: <cite data-source="paper">We call all these auxiliary wires scratch wires; their initial values may be arbitrary.</cite> When the wires carry arrays rather than single scalars, the restoration must hold address by address. Scratch storage restores its contents at every address of the [address field](address-field.md) it occupies.

The forward schedule has eight rows, and the first two are the part that deals with unknown contents. Row 0 subtracts from the target the effect that the scratch wires currently have on it, and row 1 does the same for the central wires. Rows 2 to 5 copy the source values into the scratch wires, gather them, scatter them back into the target, and then rows 6 and 7 undo the copy and the gather. The paper's lemma states the outcome: <cite data-source="paper">For arbitrary initial values of its auxiliary wires, a forward invocation changes</cite> the target by exactly the source value, and <cite data-source="paper">Each invocation restores its own auxiliary wires.</cite>

The proof treats the unknown starting values of the side and central wires as two arbitrary vectors and shows that they cancel. The first two rows subtract the effect of those starting values from the target. The later rows add back the effect of the source together with the same starting values, so the terms that depend on them appear once with each sign and vanish. The paper's count gives <cite data-source="paper">The net target change is (J V + RG)x, independently of a, c.</cite> The net change to the target therefore does not depend on the starting scratch values.

Worked example. Suppose a scratch wire starts at 7 in one run and at 0 in another, and the source value is 3 in both runs. In the first run the first row removes the 7 from the target, and the later rows add back the 7 along with the source contribution, so the net change is 3. In the second run the first row removes nothing, the later rows add back nothing extra, and the net change is again 3. The procedure's output is the same whatever the scratch wire held.

The restoration idea has an earlier precedent. <cite data-source="paper">Restoring scratch registers with arbitrary initial contents also has a precise precedent.</cite> The paper's schedule is the linear-readout version of that construction.
