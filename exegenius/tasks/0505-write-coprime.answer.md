Two integers are *coprime* when their greatest common divisor is $1$, that is, when no integer greater than $1$ divides both. A family of integers is *pairwise coprime* when every two of its members are coprime. Coprimality of the lengths lets several cyclic axes combine into one [transform axis](transform-axis.md) of their product length. The [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) rests on this fact.

For integers $\gamma$ and $\rho$, not both zero, $\gcd(\gamma,\rho)$ is the largest positive integer dividing both. For a family $f_1,\dots,f_J$ of integers, $J$ is the number of members and $f_\sigma$ is the $\sigma$-th member, with $\sigma$ running over $1,\dots,J$. The family is pairwise coprime when $\gcd(f_\sigma,f_\rho)=1$ for all $\sigma\neq\rho$. This is stronger than having no common divisor above $1$: the family $4,6,35$ has none, but $\gcd(4,6)=2$.

The paper states the requirement for its integer product as follows: <cite data-source="paper">The transform lengths needed for the integer product are pairwise coprime</cite>. The paper's lengths meet it because they are <cite data-source="paper">distinct odd primes</cite>. A prime is an integer above $1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and itself. Two distinct primes have $\gcd=1$, since a common divisor above $1$ would have to equal both.

Two facts about coprime integers finish the argument. First, if $\gcd(\gamma,\rho)=1$ and $\gamma$ and $\rho$ both divide an integer $Y$, then $\gamma\rho$ divides $Y$. [Bézout's identity](bezout-identity.md) gives integers $\xi,\zeta$ (Bézout coefficients) with $\gamma\xi+\rho\zeta=1$, so $Y=\gamma(Y\xi)+\rho(Y\zeta)$, and $\gamma\rho$ divides both terms. Second, if $\gcd(\gamma,\rho)=\gcd(\gamma',\rho)=1$, with $\gamma'$ a further integer, then $\gcd(\gamma\gamma',\rho)=1$: multiplying $\gamma\xi+\rho\zeta=1$ by $\gamma'\xi'+\rho\zeta'=1$ gives $\gamma\gamma'(\xi\xi')+\rho(\cdots)=1$.

Let $W=f_1\cdots f_J$ be the product of the family. Each $f_\sigma$ divides $W$. Conversely, if every $f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, then $W$ divides $Y$: if $f_1\cdots f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, so does $f_1\cdots f_{\sigma+1}$, by the first fact, since the second fact makes $f_1\cdots f_\sigma$ and $f_{\sigma+1}$ coprime. Hence $W$ is the least positive integer divisible by every $f_\sigma$.

Let $\mathbb{Z}$ be the integers and $\mathbb{Z}/f$ the integers modulo $f$. The transform argument uses the group $G=\mathbb{Z}/f_1\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}/f_J$, with tuples added entrywise. Let $\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)$. Then $Y\mathbf{1}=0$ exactly when every $f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, so $\mathbf{1}$ has order $W$. Since $G$ has $W$ elements, $\mathbf{1}$ generates $G$, which is therefore cyclic (one generated by a single element).

The split uses the prefix products $W_\sigma=f_1\cdots f_{\sigma-1}$, with $W_1=1$. Each $W_\sigma$ is coprime to $f_\sigma$ by the second fact, so $W_\sigma\xi+f_\sigma\zeta=1$ for some integers, and $\mu_\sigma=\xi$ satisfies $W_\sigma\mu_\sigma\equiv1\pmod{f_\sigma}$. If $Y\mu_\sigma\equiv0\pmod{f_\sigma}$ then $Y\equiv W_\sigma Y\mu_\sigma\equiv0\pmod{f_\sigma}$, so $\mu_\sigma$ has order $f_\sigma$ in $\mathbb{Z}/f_\sigma$. Hence $Y(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_J)=0$ exactly when every $f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, and the tuple $(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_J)$ has order $W$, so it generates all of $G$.
