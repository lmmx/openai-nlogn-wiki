Recursive power saving is the recurrence by which both tape procedures, chunk interchange and the simultaneous butterfly layer, cost the volume times a power below one of their parameter.

A call has a parameter $f$, the number of digits per interchanged chunk or of selected axes, and a volume $V$. A call at parameter $mf$, with $m=10^6$ (the label-space dimension) slots of size $f$, deals its rows into $W$ streams of volume $V/W$, one per wire of a fixed bit or complex [exchange network](exchange-network.md). Crossing a network edge changes the stream's frame ([Frames on wire arrays](array-frames.md)), costing one call at parameter $f$ on that stream per unit of rank of the change. So the call makes $s$ calls at parameter $f$, each on volume $V/W$, where $s$ is the total edge rank. With $F(f)$ the time per unit volume at parameter $f$, these cost $s(V/W)F(f)$, so multiplying the parameter by $m$ multiplies recursive cost per unit volume by $s/W$, less than $m$ when $s<Wm$ <cite data-source="paper">This strict inequality is the source of the power saving.</cite>

For interchange, the remaining node work (splitting, gates, shears, parking idle streams on a stack tape) is $O(V)$, and one digit swaps in linear time:

$$F(mf)\le\frac{s}{W}F(f)+O(1),\qquad F(1)=O(1).$$

Unrolling through $\log_m f$ levels ($f$ a power of $m$) gives $F(f)=O\big(\sum_{j=0}^{\log_m f}(s/W)^j\big)$. If $s/W<m^\tau$ with fixed $\tau<1$, this is $O(f^\tau)$: if $s/W\le1$ there are $1+\log_m f$ terms of at most $1$; otherwise the last term, below $m^{\tau\log_m f}=f^\tau$, dominates the geometric sum. So an interchange costs $O(Vf^\tau)$ <cite data-source="paper">The chunks can be interchanged in $O(Ve^\tau)$ time</cite>; with $s=Wm$, $O(Vf)$.

For the layer, node overhead grows: frame changes are row additions on slots of $(f/m)K$ address bits ($K$ bits per address chunk), each costing $O(V((fK)^\tau+1))$ through chunk interchanges ([Packed selected-bit addition](packed-bit-addition.md)). Recursion stops below $d^\beta$ axes, $\beta=1/2$, applying the $f$ two-point kernels singly:

$$F(f)\le\frac{s}{W}F(f/m)+O((fK)^\tau+1),\qquad F(f)=O(f)\quad(f<d^\beta).$$

Fix $\sigma<1$ with $s/W\le m^\sigma$, and $\lambda<1$ above $\sigma$ and $\tau(1+c/\beta)$, where $K\le d^c$ for a fixed exponent $c$. Internal nodes have $f\ge d^\beta$, so $K\le f^{c/\beta}$ and overhead $O(f^\lambda)$. At depth $j$ (now counting levels), the $(s/W)^j$ normalized overheads $O((f/m^j)^\lambda)$ sum geometrically, with ratio $(s/W)/m^\lambda<1$, to $O(f^\lambda)$; leaves contribute $(s/W)^jf/m^j\le f^\sigma(f/m^j)^{1-\sigma}<f^\sigma d^{\beta(1-\sigma)}$. With $f\le d$, and a $\log d$ factor from splitting into powers of $m$, the layer costs $O(Vd^{\lambda'})$ for fixed $\lambda'$ between $\max\{\lambda,\sigma+\beta(1-\sigma)\}$ and $1$ <cite data-source="paper">\max\{\lambda,\sigma+\beta(1-\sigma)\}<\lambda'<1.</cite>

The recursion gets its strict inequality $s<Wm$ from [Nested subspace labels and rank deficit](subspace-labels.md): an edge's rank is the dimension change of nested subspace labels, so a wire climbing from $0$ to $m$ dimensions costs $m$. One data bank's $N$ wires start on a line and the other's stop one dimension short, saving $2N$; the bit network spends $N$ on extra source ranks, and shrinking edges cost back $2L<N$.

Write $s/W=m(1-\eta)$, with $\eta$ now the relative deficit $(Wm-s)/(Wm)$, which is $339/22587335000000$ for the bit network and $73/19906842167500$ for the complex one <cite data-source="paper">=\frac{339}{22587335000000}</cite>. Both, about $1.5\cdot10^{-11}$ and $3.7\cdot10^{-12}$, exceed $20\cdot2^{-50}\approx1.8\cdot10^{-14}$ <cite data-source="paper">Both exact deficits displayed above are greater than $20\cdot2^{-50}$.</cite> Since $m<2^{20}$, $\ln m<20\ln 2<20$; as the exponential lies above its tangent at $0$, $m^{1-2^{-50}}=m\exp(-2^{-50}\ln m)>m(1-2^{-50}\ln m)>m(1-20\cdot2^{-50})>m(1-\eta)=s/W$ for either network. Hence $\tau=\sigma=1-2^{-50}$ gives $s/W<m^\tau$ for the bit network and $s/W<m^\sigma$ for the complex one, exponents barely below $1$.
