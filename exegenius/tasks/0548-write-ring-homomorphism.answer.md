A ring homomorphism is a map between rings that preserves addition, multiplication and $1$; it is an isomorphism when it is also bijective (one-to-one and onto), so the two rings have the same structure. The paper's map $\Phi$ is an isomorphism, which lets a convolution be computed as a product in another ring.

Let $R$ and $R'$ be rings. A map $\sigma: R\to R'$ is a ring homomorphism when the following hold for all $a,b\in R$:
$$\sigma(a+b)=\sigma(a)+\sigma(b),\qquad \sigma(ab)=\sigma(a)\sigma(b),\qquad \sigma(1)=1.$$
An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism. Its inverse also preserves products, since $\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(u)\sigma^{-1}(v))=uv$ and $\sigma$ is one-to-one.

Let $W=\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^2-1)$ be the quotient ring of complex polynomials in an indeterminate $t$, in which every multiple of $t^2-1$ counts as zero. On this page $t$ denotes only this indeterminate. Each element of $W$ is $\alpha+\beta t$ for unique complex numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$, its coefficients, because a nonzero polynomial of degree below 2 is never a multiple of $t^2-1$.

Let $s$ be an element of a commutative ring $R'$ containing $\mathbb{C}$, with $s^2=1$. Then $\alpha+\beta t\mapsto\alpha+\beta s$ is a ring homomorphism $W\to R'$, because substitution preserves sums and products of polynomials and sends $t^2-1$ to $s^2-1=0$. Conversely, a homomorphism $W\to R'$ that fixes $\mathbb{C}$ is determined by the image $s$ of $t$, and that image must satisfy $s^2=1$.

Let $W'=\mathbb{C}[y]/(y^2+1)$, where $y$ is an indeterminate with $y^2=-1$; every element of $W'$ is uniquely $u+vy$ with $u,v\in\mathbb{C}$. Let $\mathrm{i}$ be the imaginary unit. Since $(\mathrm{i}y)^2=1$, the rule $\sigma(\alpha+\beta t)=\alpha+\beta\,\mathrm{i}y$ is a homomorphism $\sigma: W\to W'$. It is bijective: $\sigma(\alpha+\beta t)=u+vy$ exactly when $\alpha=u$ and $\beta=-\mathrm{i}v$. Its inverse sends $1+y$ to $1-\mathrm{i}t$, so $\sigma$ is an isomorphism. As a check, $(1+t)^2=2+2t$ in $W$ (as $t^2=1$), and $(1+\mathrm{i}y)^2=2+2\mathrm{i}y=\sigma(2+2t)$ in $W'$.

Let $\rho: W\to\mathbb{C}$ be $\rho(\alpha+\beta t)=\alpha+\beta$, the substitution $t\mapsto 1$; here $\rho$ names only this map. It is a homomorphism by the lemma above but not one-to-one, since $t-1\neq 0$ in $W$ while $\rho(t-1)=0$. No isomorphism $W\to\mathbb{C}$ exists, however: $(t-1)(t+1)=0$ in $W$ with both factors nonzero, while an isomorphism $\theta$ would send them to nonzero numbers whose product is $\theta(0)=0$, which is impossible in $\mathbb{C}$. So $W$ and $\mathbb{C}$ do not have the same structure.

Let $S=s_1\cdots s_d$, where the $s_i$ are pairwise coprime, and let $x$ be the generator of the first ring. The paper's map $\Phi$ sends $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^S-1)$ to $\mathbb{C}[x_1,\dots,x_d]/(x_1^{s_1}-1,\dots,x_d^{s_d}-1)$. $\Phi$ sends $x$ to the monomial $g=\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i}$, where $\mu_i$ is the paper's integer with $P_i\mu_i\equiv 1 \pmod{s_i}$ and $P_i=s_1\cdots s_{i-1}$, with $P_1=1$. The paper checks that this is well defined with <cite data-source="paper">respects xS = 1, so it defines an algebra map.</cite> Substituting $g$ for $x$ gives a homomorphism as in the lemma, and $g^S=1$ because each $s_i$ divides $S$.

Bijectivity is verified using a [basis](linear-algebra-basics.md) of the target. The monomials $x_1^{e_1}\cdots x_d^{e_d}$, where $e_i$ is the exponent of $x_i$ and $0\le e_i<s_i$, form a basis of the target, and there are $S$ of them. The power $g^k$ is the basis monomial with exponents $k\mu_i \bmod s_i$. If $g^k=g^{k'}$ with $0\le k,k'<S$, then $s_i\mid(k-k')\mu_i$ for each $i$, so $s_i\mid k-k'$ because $\mu_i$ is invertible modulo $s_i$, and so $S\mid k-k'$ by pairwise coprimality. Hence the powers of $g$ run <cite data-source="paper">through all target basis monomials exactly once.</cite> So $\Phi$ maps the basis $1,x,\dots,x^{S-1}$ of the source onto the target basis, and it is bijective as well as a homomorphism.

The paper's statement is <cite data-source="paper">Hence Φ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic</cite>. An isomorphism of this kind [preserves cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md). Identify a sequence $(a_k)$ of length $S$ with $a=\sum_k a_kx^k$, and likewise $b$. The product $ab$ is the cyclic convolution of $a$ and $b$, so $\Phi(ab)=\Phi(a)\Phi(b)$ and $ab=\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(a)\Phi(b))$.
