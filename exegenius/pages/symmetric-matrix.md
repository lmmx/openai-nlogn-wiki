A *symmetric matrix* is a square matrix equal to its own transpose. A symmetric matrix is a special case of the matrices in [Linear algebra basics](linear-algebra-basics.md). The property matters for the side wires of the [Subset-intersection network](subset-intersection-network.md): <cite data-source="paper">A side wire still joins neighboring physical triples in stage 2</cite>, because the neighbour relation is symmetric.

The *transpose* $A^{\top}$ of an $m\times m$ matrix $A$ is the $m\times m$ matrix whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $A_{ji}$, so its rows are the columns of $A$. The matrix $A$ is *symmetric* when $A^{\top}=A$, that is, when $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$ for all $i$ and $j$ from $1$ to $m$.

Take $m=2$ over the real numbers. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric, because its entries in positions $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ are both $2$. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ is not, because its entry in row $1$, column $2$ is $2$ while its entry in row $2$, column $1$ is $0$. Diagonal entries are compared only with themselves, so they may take any values.

Let $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ be three-element subsets of a finite set. They are *neighbours* when they share exactly one element, which the paper writes as <cite data-source="paper">neighbors when |S ∩ T | = 1</cite>, that is, $|\sigma\cap\sigma'|=1$. Let $A$ be the table whose entry at $(\sigma,\sigma')$ is $1$ when they are neighbours and $0$ otherwise. Its rows and columns are indexed by the same set of triples, and it is symmetric: $|\sigma\cap\sigma'|=|\sigma'\cap\sigma|$, so $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ are neighbours exactly when $\sigma'$ and $\sigma$ are, and $A_{\sigma\sigma'}=A_{\sigma'\sigma}$. For example, $\{1,2,3\}$ and $\{3,4,5\}$ share only $3$, so both entries are $1$. The triples $\{1,2,3\}$ and $\{1,2,4\}$ share two elements, so both entries are $0$.
