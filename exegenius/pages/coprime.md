Two integers are *coprime* when their greatest common divisor is $1$: no integer above $1$ divides both. A family of integers is *pairwise coprime* when every two of its members are coprime. Coprimality of the lengths lets several cyclic axes combine into one [transform axis](transform-axis.md) of their product length. The [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) rests on this fact.

One integer divides another when the second is a multiple of the first. Here $\gamma$ and $\rho$ are integers; the letters change meaning from their scalar and exponent uses on other pages. For integers $\gamma$ and $\rho$, not both zero, $\gcd(\gamma,\rho)$ is the largest positive integer dividing both. For a family $f_1,\dots,f_J$ of integers, $J$ is the number of members and $f_\sigma$ is the $\sigma$-th member, with $\sigma$ running over $1,\dots,J$ (the letters $f$ and $\sigma$ change meaning here). The family is pairwise coprime when $\gcd(f_\sigma,f_{\sigma'})=1$ for all distinct indices $\sigma,\sigma'$. This is stronger than having no common divisor above $1$: the family $4,6,35$ has none, yet $\gcd(4,6)=2$.

The paper states the requirement for its integer product: <cite data-source="paper">The transform lengths needed for the integer product are pairwise coprime</cite>. Its lengths are <cite data-source="paper">distinct odd primes</cite>. A prime is an integer above $1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and itself, so two distinct primes are coprime. The paper checks each prime length against its power-of-two box: <cite data-source="paper">Every gcd(si , ti ) equals one because ti is a power of two.</cite>

Two facts follow. First, if $\gamma$ and $\rho$ both divide an integer $Y$ and $\gcd(\gamma,\rho)=1$, then $\gamma\rho$ divides $Y$. [Bézout's identity](bezout-identity.md) gives integers $\Xi,\Psi$ (Bézout coefficients) with $\gamma\Xi+\rho\Psi=1$, so $Y=\gamma(Y\Xi)+\rho(Y\Psi)$. Since $\rho$ divides $Y$, it divides $Y\Xi$; since $\gamma$ divides $Y$, it divides $Y\Psi$. So $\gamma\rho$ divides both terms, hence $Y$. Second, if $\gcd(\gamma,\rho)=\gcd(\gamma',\rho)=1$ for a further integer $\gamma'$, then $\gcd(\gamma\gamma',\rho)=1$. With $\gamma'\Xi'+\rho\Psi'=1$ for integers $\Xi',\Psi'$, multiplying the two identities gives $\gamma\gamma'(\Xi\Xi')+\rho(\cdots)=1$.

Let $\Pi=f_1\cdots f_J$ be the product of the family. Each $f_\sigma$ divides $\Pi$. Conversely, if every $f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, then $\Pi$ divides $Y$: if $f_1\cdots f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, so does $f_1\cdots f_{\sigma+1}$, by the first fact, since the second makes $f_1\cdots f_\sigma$ coprime to $f_{\sigma+1}$. So $\Pi$ is the least positive integer that every $f_\sigma$ divides.

Let $\mathbb{Z}/f$ be the integers modulo $f$, whose classes hold integers differing by multiples of $f$. Let $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}/f_1\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}/f_J$ be the group of tuples under entrywise addition; it has $\Pi$ elements. Let $\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)$. The *order* of an element is the least positive integer $m$ for which $m$ times the element is $0$. Now $Y\mathbf{1}=0$ exactly when every $f_\sigma$ divides $Y$, that is, when $\Pi$ divides $Y$. So $\mathbf{1}$ has order $\Pi$, its $\Pi$ multiples are all of $\Gamma$, and $\Gamma$ is *cyclic*, generated by one element.

The split uses the prefix products $\Pi_\sigma=f_1\cdots f_{\sigma-1}$, with $\Pi_1=1$. Each $\Pi_\sigma$ is coprime to $f_\sigma$, so $\Pi_\sigma\Xi+f_\sigma\Psi=1$ for some integers, and $\Upsilon_\sigma=\Xi$ satisfies $\Pi_\sigma\Upsilon_\sigma\equiv1\pmod{f_\sigma}$. Thus $f_\sigma$ divides $Y\Upsilon_\sigma$ exactly when it divides $Y$, since $Y\equiv\Pi_\sigma Y\Upsilon_\sigma\pmod{f_\sigma}$. So $\Upsilon_\sigma$ has order $f_\sigma$ in $\mathbb{Z}/f_\sigma$, and the argument for $\mathbf{1}$ shows that $(\Upsilon_1,\dots,\Upsilon_J)$ has order $\Pi$, so it generates $\Gamma$.
