Concept: Radix-digit convolution
Gloss: Writing both integers as polynomials in a power of two whose coefficients are their digits, so that their product is a polynomial convolution of short coefficients.

Radix-digit convolution is a reduction of [exact integer multiplication](exact-integer-multiplication.md) that writes each input as a polynomial in a power of two whose coefficients are its digits, so that the product becomes a convolution of short integer coefficients. It is the first step of the argument for [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md): the [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) and [carry propagation](carry-propagation.md) pages build on the coefficients it produces.

The paper states the reduction in one sentence: <cite data-source="paper">Splitting the inputs into radix digits reduces multiplication to convolution of short integer coefficients.</cite>

The input length $n$ is the number of bits in each input. Let $x$ and $y$ be the two inputs, each a binary string of $n$ bits. Fix a digit width $b$: each digit is a block of $b$ bits. The paper takes <cite data-source="paper">b = ⌈log2 n⌉</cite>, so that $2^b \ge n$. Split each input into blocks of $b$ bits, counting from the least significant end. A block is a digit in base $2^b$, the radix: its value lies between $0$ and $2^b - 1$. The inputs have $q = \lceil n/b \rceil$ digits each. Here $i$ is the position of a digit, running from $0$ to $q-1$. The digits of $x$ are $x_0$ through $x_{q-1}$, where $x_i$ is the digit of weight $(2^b)^i$, so that $x = \sum_i x_i (2^b)^i$. The digits of $y$ are $y_0$ through $y_{q-1}$, with $y_i$ defined the same way.

Here $z$ is a formal variable, and each input becomes a polynomial in $z$ whose coefficients are its digits. Substituting $z = 2^b$ into that polynomial returns the input.

Multiplying the two digit polynomials gives a polynomial of degree at most $2q-2$. Here $j$ is the position of a product coefficient, running from $0$ to $2q-2$. The coefficient $c_j$ is the integer multiplying $z^j$ in the product of the two digit polynomials. It is the sum
$$c_j = \sum_i x_i\, y_{j-i},$$
taken over all $i$ with $0 \le i \le q-1$ and $0 \le j-i \le q-1$. This sum is the convolution of the digit sequences $(x_i)$ and $(y_i)$. Substituting $z = 2^b$ into the product gives the identity $xy = \sum_j c_j (2^b)^j$, so the integer product is recovered from the sequence $(c_j)$ once the coefficients are known.

Each $c_j$ is a sum of at most $q$ products $x_i\, y_{j-i}$, and each product is at most $(2^b-1)^2$, so $0 \le c_j \le q(2^b-1)^2$. Since $q \le n \le 2^b$, every coefficient is below $2^{3b}$, so it fits in $3b$ bits. <cite data-source="paper">Every product coefficient obeys</cite> this bound.

Take $n = 4$. Then $b = 2$ and $q = 2$. Let $x = 1101$ (value 13) and $y = 1011$ (value 11). Splitting into base-4 digits from the least significant end gives $x_0 = 1$, $x_1 = 3$, $y_0 = 3$ and $y_1 = 2$. The coefficients are $c_0 = x_0 y_0 = 3$, $c_1 = x_0 y_1 + x_1 y_0 = 2 + 9 = 11$ and $c_2 = x_1 y_1 = 6$. Check: $3 + 11 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 16 = 143 = 13 \cdot 11$. The coefficient $c_1 = 11$ is larger than a digit, so the sequence is not yet the binary product. Carry propagation converts it into one.

The reason for the split is cost. Each coefficient has $O(\log n)$ bits, so arithmetic on a single coefficient is cheap, and the convolution is no longer a problem about individual bits. Its work is set by how the transforms move the $2q-1$ coefficients. The transforms are the expensive part, since each transform level scans the stored bits. The paper's count of this is that <cite data-source="paper">Θ(log n) transform levels already costs Θ(n log n).</cite> The later pages reduce that cost.
