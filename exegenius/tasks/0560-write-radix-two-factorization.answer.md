Radix-two factorization splits the [Discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) of length $q$ into $\lg q$ layers, when $q$ is a power of two. Its layers are built from [Butterfly](butterfly.md) maps on pairs of entries, so the whole transform costs on the order of $q\lg q$ operations instead of the $q^2$ of the defining matrix product. This is what lets the Fourier transforms of the convolution be replaced by a [Multidimensional transform](multidimensional-transform.md) whose axis lengths are powers of two.

Let $\omega = e^{-2\pi i/q}$, so that $\omega^{q/2} = -1$, and $(F_q u)_\ell = \frac{1}{q}\sum_{s=0}^{q-1}\omega^{\ell s}u_s$ for $0 \le \ell < q$. Let $\sigma$ be the output index of a half-length transform, with $0 \le \sigma < q/2$. Each output index $\ell$ then has the form $\ell = 2\sigma + b$, where $b \in \{0,1\}$ is the index bit, the lowest bit of $\ell$.

Split the sum over $s$ into the terms with $s < q/2$ and the terms with $s + q/2$. Since $\omega^{\ell(s+q/2)} = (-1)^{\ell}\omega^{\ell s}$, and $(-1)^{2\sigma+b} = (-1)^b$, while $\omega^{(2\sigma+b)s} = (\omega^2)^{\sigma s}\,\omega^{bs}$, this gives

$$(F_q u)_{2\sigma+b} = \frac{1}{2}\,\Bigl(F_{q/2}\bigl(\omega^{bs}\,(u_s + (-1)^b u_{s+q/2})\bigr)_{0\le s<q/2}\Bigr)_{\sigma}.$$

Here $F_{q/2}$ is the same normalized transform of length $q/2$, with $\omega^2$ in place of $\omega$. The factor $\frac12$ appears because the sum over $s < q/2$ carries $\frac{1}{q} = \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{q/2}$.

Read as a procedure, one layer does the following. For each $0 \le s < q/2$, apply the butterfly $H_0$ to the pair $(u_s, u_{s+q/2})$, multiply the second output by $\omega^{s}$, and store the first output in array position $s$ and the second in position $s+q/2$. <cite data-source="paper">The two outputs of H0 occupy these same slots</cite>. The two halves of the array, positions $0$ to $q/2-1$ and $q/2$ to $q-1$, are then transformed by the same procedure at length $q/2$. The first half gives the entries with even $\ell$, and the second half gives the entries with odd $\ell$. <cite data-source="paper">The length-one procedure is the identity.</cite> So the recursion ends when every piece has length one.

Each layer uses $q/2$ butterflies and at most $q/2$ phase multiplications, and there are $\lg q$ layers. The layers therefore cost $O(q\lg q)$ operations. The paper counts these as <cite data-source="paper">a = log2 t levels</cite>. Let $\mathrm{rev}(\ell)$ be the integer whose $\lg q$ binary digits are those of $\ell$ in reverse order. The entries come out in bit-reversed order: after $\lg q$ levels the entry $(F_q u)_\ell$ sits in array position $\mathrm{rev}(\ell)$. This is the [Bit-reversed index order](bit-reversed-order.md). <cite data-source="paper">the entry (Ft− z)j occupies slot reva (j)</cite>

For example, take $q = 4$ and $u = (1,2,3,4)$, so $\omega = -i$. The first layer pairs $(u_0,u_2) = (1,3)$ and $(u_1,u_3) = (2,4)$. The first outputs are $(1+3)/2 = 2$ and $(2+4)/2 = 3$. The second outputs are $(1-3)/2 = -1$ and $\omega\,(2-4)/2 = i$. The array after this layer is $(2,3,-1,i)$. The half $(2,3)$ transforms to $(2.5,\,-0.5)$, which are $(F_4 u)_0$ and $(F_4 u)_2$. The half $(-1,i)$ transforms to $(-0.5+0.5i,\,-0.5-0.5i)$, which are $(F_4 u)_1$ and $(F_4 u)_3$. The final array is $(2.5,\,-0.5,\,-0.5+0.5i,\,-0.5-0.5i)$, which is the transform in bit-reversed order.
