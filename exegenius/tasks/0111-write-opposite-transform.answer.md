The opposite transform $F_q^+$ is the [Discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) that reverses the sign in its exponent. It is the step that turns frequency-domain products back into coefficients, so the convolution pages use it as the final step after a pointwise product.

For a vector $u$ of $q$ complex numbers, with entries indexed by $j$ and $k$ from $0$ to $q-1$, the opposite transform is
$$(F_q^+ u)_k = \frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1} \omega^{-jk}\, u_j, \qquad \omega = e^{-2\pi i/q}.$$
The forward transform $F_q$ uses $\omega^{jk}$ in place of $\omega^{-jk}$. The opposite transform therefore reverses the sign in the exponent, which is the same as replacing $\omega$ by $\omega^{-1} = e^{2\pi i/q}$; the source describes this as <cite data-source="paper">conjugation changes the negative Fourier sign to the positive one</cite>. The factor $1/q$ is the normalization: each output is an average of $q$ terms, and the source notes that <cite data-source="paper">each output averages inputs multiplied by complex numbers of modulus one</cite>, so the transform does not enlarge the size of its input.

The two transforms undo each other up to the factor $q$. Take $q = 4$, so $\omega = -i$ and $\omega^{-1} = i$. For the input $u = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, the forward transform gives $F_4 u = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$. Applying the opposite transform to this $v = F_4 u$ gives
$$(F_4^+ v)_k = \frac{1}{16}\sum_{j=0}^{3} i^{jk}.$$
For $k = 0$ the sum is $4$, so the entry is $\tfrac14$. For $k = 1, 2, 3$ the four terms cancel in pairs or sum to zero, for example $1 + i - 1 - i = 0$ when $k = 1$. The result is $(\tfrac14, 0, 0, 0) = u/4$. In general $F_q^+ F_q u = u/q$, which is the sense in which the opposite transform inverts the forward one.

The same sign convention is used on the synthetic transforms of the paper, whose entries are polynomials rather than complex numbers. There the forward transforms of two inputs are multiplied pointwise, and the opposite transform of the product gives the convolution: <cite data-source="paper">Two normalized synthetic forwards, pointwise polynomial multiplication divided by r, and the normalized opposite transform give</cite> the normalized product. The paper also requires that the product keep its frequency order, since <cite data-source="paper">the opposite transform consumes that same order after a pointwise product.</cite>
