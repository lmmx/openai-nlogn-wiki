Schoolbook multiplication is the grade-school method for multiplying two integers digit by digit. It is the base case of the paper's construction: the final bound needs its cost for small inputs.

For $n$-bit binary strings $x$ and $y$, the method goes through the bits of $y$. Each bit $1$ adds a copy of $x$ shifted left to that bit's position; each bit $0$ adds nothing. The $n$ shifted copies are then summed. Each has at most about $n$ bits and there are $n$ of them, so the method takes $O(n^2)$ bit operations, quadratic in the number of digits. Worked example: $3 \times 5$ in binary is $11 \times 101$. Bit $1$ at position $0$ gives $11$. Bit $0$ at position $1$ gives $0$. Bit $1$ at position $2$ gives $11$ shifted two places left, which is $1100$. Adding the aligned copies gives $0011 + 0000 + 1100 = 1111$, which is $15$.

Schoolbook multiplication is a method for [Exact integer multiplication](exact-integer-multiplication.md): it outputs the full product of two $n$-bit inputs, and it is exact for every input.

In the bound below, $\lg$ is the base-2 logarithm and $n$ is the input length. Here $\kappa = 2^{-182}$ is a fixed constant, so $(\lg n)^{1-\kappa}$ grows slightly more slowly than $\lg n$. The paper's large-input bound is $O(n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa})$. The construction runs with a precision $p$ and a logical bit volume. The parameters are chosen so that the logical bit volume is $O(n)$ and $p = O(\lg n)$. The bound holds for inputs at or above a cutoff. <cite data-source="paper">Fix one cutoff beyond all the size, precision, margin and layout conditions, and use schoolbook</cite> multiplication for inputs shorter than it. Below the cutoff there are finitely many lengths, each with a fixed cost, so their largest cost is a constant $C$. Since $n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa} \ge 1$ for $n \ge 1$, we have $C \le C\,n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa}$. <cite data-source="paper">The finitely many smaller lengths are included by enlarging the implicit</cite> constant. So the small cases cost only a constant factor more, and the $O(n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa})$ bound holds for every input length.
