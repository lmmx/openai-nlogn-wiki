A ring homomorphism is a map between rings that preserves sums, products and the element $1$, and an isomorphism is one that is also bijective (one-to-one and onto), so the two rings have the same structure. The paper's map $\Phi$ is such an isomorphism, which turns a [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md) into an ordinary product in another ring; the phase twist and the convolution over the polynomial ring build on this step.

Let $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{S}$ be rings. A map $\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{S}$ is a ring homomorphism when, for all $\xi,\zeta\in\mathcal{R}$,
$$\mathcal{H}(\xi+\zeta)=\mathcal{H}(\xi)+\mathcal{H}(\zeta),\qquad \mathcal{H}(\xi\zeta)=\mathcal{H}(\xi)\mathcal{H}(\zeta),\qquad \mathcal{H}(1)=1.$$
An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism. Its inverse is also a homomorphism: for $\xi,\zeta\in\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\xi)\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\zeta))=\xi\zeta$, and applying $\mathcal{H}^{-1}$ to both sides gives $\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\xi\zeta)=\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\xi)\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\zeta)$.

An indeterminate $\mathcal{X}$ is a formal variable, not a number. Let $\mathcal{Q}_1=\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{X}]/(\mathcal{X}^2-1)$, the quotient ring of complex polynomials in which every multiple of $\mathcal{X}^2-1$ counts as zero. A nonzero polynomial of degree below 2 is never such a multiple, so each element of $\mathcal{Q}_1$ is uniquely a polynomial of degree below 2 in $\mathcal{X}$. Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a commutative ring (multiplication order does not matter) containing $\mathbb{C}$, and let $\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{S}$ satisfy $\mathcal{U}^2=1$. Substituting $\mathcal{U}$ for $\mathcal{X}$ is a ring homomorphism $\mathcal{Q}_1\to\mathcal{S}$: substitution preserves sums and products of polynomials, and it sends $\mathcal{X}^2-1$ to $\mathcal{U}^2-1=0$. Let $\mathcal{Y}$ be a second indeterminate. The same holds for $\mathcal{Q}_2=\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{Y}]/(\mathcal{Y}^2+1)$ with $\mathcal{U}^2=-1$.

**Example 1 (an isomorphism).** Let $\mathrm{i}$ be the imaginary unit, and let $\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{Q}_1\to\mathcal{Q}_2$ send $\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathrm{i}\mathcal{Y}$, allowed since $(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{Y})^2=(-1)(-1)=1$. Let $\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{Q}_2\to\mathcal{Q}_1$ send $\mathcal{Y}\mapsto-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{X}$, allowed since $(-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{X})^2=-1$. Then $\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X}))=\mathrm{i}\cdot(-\mathrm{i})\,\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}$, and likewise $\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{Y}))=\mathcal{Y}$. Both composites are homomorphisms fixing $\mathbb{C}$, so each fixes every polynomial in its indeterminate; hence $\mathcal{H}$ is bijective with inverse $\mathcal{J}$. As a check, $(1+\mathcal{X})^2=2+2\mathcal{X}$ in $\mathcal{Q}_1$, and $\mathcal{H}(2+2\mathcal{X})=2+2\mathrm{i}\mathcal{Y}=(1+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{Y})^2$.

**Example 2 (not an isomorphism).** Let $\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{Q}_1\to\mathbb{C}$ send $\mathcal{X}\mapsto1$, allowed since $1^2=1$. Then $\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{X}-1)=0$ while $\mathcal{X}-1\neq0$ in $\mathcal{Q}_1$, so $\mathcal{E}$ is not one-to-one. In fact no isomorphism $\mathcal{Q}_1\to\mathbb{C}$ exists: $(\mathcal{X}-1)(\mathcal{X}+1)=0$ with both factors nonzero, but an injective homomorphism $\theta$ sends them to nonzero numbers whose product is $\theta(0)=0$, which is impossible in $\mathbb{C}$. So $\mathcal{Q}_1$ and $\mathbb{C}$ do not have the same structure.

**Example 3 (the paper's map).** Let $\mathcal{Q}_3=\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{X}]/(\mathcal{X}^6-1)$, and let $\mathcal{Z}_1$ and $\mathcal{Z}_2$ be the indeterminates of the cyclic axes of lengths 2 and 3, so the target is $\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{Z}_1,\mathcal{Z}_2]/(\mathcal{Z}_1^2-1,\mathcal{Z}_2^3-1)$. The paper's map $\Phi$ sends $\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{Z}_1\mathcal{Z}_2^{2}$. Each exponent is the inverse, modulo that axis length, of the product of the earlier lengths: the first axis has no earlier length, and for the axis of length 3 the product is 2, with $2^{-1}\equiv 2 \pmod 3$. Since $(\mathcal{Z}_1\mathcal{Z}_2^2)^6=(\mathcal{Z}_1^2)^3(\mathcal{Z}_2^3)^4=1$, the assignment is a homomorphism as in the lemma; the paper states that it <cite data-source="paper">respects xS = 1, so it defines an algebra map.</cite>

Bijectivity is verified through a [basis](linear-algebra-basics.md) of the target. The six monomials $\mathcal{Z}_1^{e_1}\mathcal{Z}_2^{e_2}$ with $e_1\in\{0,1\}$ and $e_2\in\{0,1,2\}$ form a basis. Using $\mathcal{Z}_1^2=1$ and $\mathcal{Z}_2^3=1$, the powers of the image of $\mathcal{X}$ are $\Phi(\mathcal{X})^0=1$, $\Phi(\mathcal{X})^1=\mathcal{Z}_1\mathcal{Z}_2^2$, $\Phi(\mathcal{X})^2=\mathcal{Z}_2$, $\Phi(\mathcal{X})^3=\mathcal{Z}_1$, $\Phi(\mathcal{X})^4=\mathcal{Z}_2^2$ and $\Phi(\mathcal{X})^5=\mathcal{Z}_1\mathcal{Z}_2$. These six monomials are distinct, so the powers <cite data-source="paper">through all target basis monomials exactly once.</cite> Thus $\Phi$ maps the basis $1,\mathcal{X},\dots,\mathcal{X}^5$ of $\mathcal{Q}_3$ onto the target basis, so it is bijective, and hence an isomorphism. The paper's conclusion reads <cite data-source="paper">Hence Φ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic</cite>.

Identify a length-6 sequence with the element of $\mathcal{Q}_3$ whose coefficients it lists. The product of two such elements $\xi,\zeta$ is their cyclic convolution, and $\Phi(\xi\zeta)=\Phi(\xi)\Phi(\zeta)$. Since $\Phi$ is an isomorphism, the [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md) of two sequences reduces to a product in the target ring, and applying $\Phi^{-1}$ recovers the convolution from that product.
