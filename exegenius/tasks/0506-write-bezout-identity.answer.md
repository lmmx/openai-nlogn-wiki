Bézout's identity says that the greatest common divisor of two integers is an integer combination of them. It supplies the [modular inverses](modular-inverse.md) that the [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) needs when its lengths are coprime.

Let $\beta$ and $\gamma$ be integers, not both zero. The identity is stated in terms of the greatest common divisor $\gcd(\beta,\gamma)$, the largest positive integer dividing both, which the [Coprime](coprime.md) page defines. It asserts that there are integers $\rho$ and $\sigma$, the Bézout coefficients, with
$$\beta\rho+\gamma\sigma=\gcd(\beta,\gamma).$$

The proof also shows how to find $\rho$ and $\sigma$. Let $\rho'$ and $\sigma'$ be any integers, not yet known to be the coefficients. The positive values of $\beta\rho'+\gamma\sigma'$ form a nonempty set, since $\beta\beta+\gamma\gamma>0$, so the set has a least element $\xi$. Divide $\beta$ by $\xi$: let $f$ be the integer quotient and $\varepsilon$ the remainder, with $0\le\varepsilon<\xi$, so that $\beta=f\xi+\varepsilon$. Then $\varepsilon=\beta(1-f\rho')+\gamma(-f\sigma')$ has the same form, and it is smaller than $\xi$, so minimality forces $\varepsilon=0$. Thus $\xi$ divides $\beta$, and the same argument gives $\xi\mid\gamma$. Hence $\xi\le\gcd(\beta,\gamma)$. Conversely, $\gcd(\beta,\gamma)$ divides $\beta\rho'+\gamma\sigma'=\xi$, so $\gcd(\beta,\gamma)\le\xi$. Therefore $\xi=\gcd(\beta,\gamma)$, and the trial integers are the coefficients: $\rho=\rho'$ and $\sigma=\sigma'$.

For $\beta=3$ and $\gamma=5$ we have $\gcd(3,5)=1$ and $3\cdot 2+5\cdot(-1)=1$, so $\rho=2$ and $\sigma=-1$. The coefficients are not unique. Let $t$ be an arbitrary integer, and let each value of $t$ label one pair of coefficients: $\rho=2+5t$ and $\sigma=-1-3t$ also work for every integer $t$, because $3(2+5t)+5(-1-3t)=1$. For $\beta=12$ and $\gamma=18$ we have $\gcd(12,18)=6$ and $12\cdot(-1)+18\cdot 1=6$, so the combination equals the greatest common divisor, not $1$.

When $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are coprime, $\gcd(\beta,\gamma)=1$, so $\beta\rho\equiv 1 \pmod{\gamma}$ and $\rho$ is an inverse of $\beta$ modulo $\gamma$. For $3$ and $5$ this gives $\rho=2$, since $6\equiv 1 \pmod 5$.

The paper reduces rational coefficients, which are fractions of integers whose denominators are coprime to a prime-power modulus, <cite data-source="paper">by the extended Euclidean algorithm using ordinary tape arithmetic</cite>. The extended Euclidean algorithm computes Bézout coefficients, so it supplies the inverse of each denominator: for $\tfrac13$ modulo $5$ the inverse is $2$, so $\tfrac13$ reduces to $2$.
