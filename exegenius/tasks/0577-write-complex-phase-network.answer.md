The complex phase network is built on the [subset-intersection network](subset-intersection-network.md), with each wire carrying an array of complex values instead of a scalar. <cite data-source="paper">replace the scalar on each wire w by an array</cite>. Its edges change the frames of these arrays by products of simple two-point kernels, and this identity is what the [simultaneous butterfly layer](simultaneous-butterfly-layer.md) uses to apply a butterfly on several address bits in one pass, one step toward the [main theorem](main-theorem.md).

The complex phase network takes its addresses from the [binary field](binary-field.md), in which addition is exclusive or. Let $m$ be the number of coordinates of an address, so addresses are the vectors $x\in\mathbb F_2^m$, and each array has $2^m$ entries, one per address. For a binary vector $z$, $\mathrm{supp}(z)$ is the set of coordinates at which $z$ is $1$, and $\mathrm{wt}(z)=|\mathrm{supp}(z)|$ is its Hamming weight, the number of such coordinates read as an integer. For binary vectors $u$ and $v$, the dot product $u\cdot v$ is the parity of $|\mathrm{supp}(u)\cap\mathrm{supp}(v)|$. For $b\in\mathbb F_2$, $[b]\in\{0,1\}$ is its integer representative.

The complex phase network is indexed by [subspace labels](subspace-labels.md). Write $A\perp B$ when every vector of $A$ is orthogonal to every vector of $B$ and $A\cap B=0$, so that $A+B$ is their orthogonal sum. Each vertex carries one label $U$, a subspace of $\mathbb F_2^m$ with $U\cap U^\perp=0$, where $U^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement; such a subspace is nondegenerate. <cite data-source="paper">Use the complex scalar network and binary labels.</cite> The complex phase network checks nondegeneracy with the [Gram matrix](gram-matrix.md) of each label, the matrix of dot products of a basis of $U$. For a nondegenerate subspace $W$, $P_W$ is the projection onto $W$ along $W^\perp$.

The complex phase network computes its frames by the [Tensor product](tensor-product.md) $H^{\otimes m}$ of $H=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, the normalized Hadamard matrix; here $H$ denotes this matrix, not the address chunk of other pages. The phase exponent of an address $x$ is $q_U(x)=\mathrm{wt}(P_Ux)\bmod 4$. For a function $f$ on addresses, $\mathrm{diag}_x(f(x))$ is the diagonal operator that multiplies the value at each address $x$ by $f(x)$. The frame at a vertex with label $U$, the operator $C_U$, is
$$C_U = H^{\otimes m}\,\mathrm{diag}_x\big(i^{q_U(x)}\big)\,H^{\otimes m},$$
where $i$ is the imaginary unit.

The Kronecker product obeys the mixed-product rule $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)$ for matrices of compatible sizes. Since $H^2=I$, it gives $(H^{\otimes m})^2=I$. Each entry of $H^{\otimes m}$ is $\pm 2^{-m/2}$, so each entry of $C_U$ is $2^{-m}$ times a sum of terms $\pm i^{q_U(x)}$, which is a Gaussian integer. Since $H^{\otimes m}$ is its own inverse, $C_U^{-1}=H^{\otimes m}\,\mathrm{diag}_x\big(i^{-q_U(x)}\big)\,H^{\otimes m}$, and the same argument applies to it. The complex phase network has coefficients in [Gaussian dyadics](gaussian-dyadics.md), since each such entry is a Gaussian integer divided by $2^m$.

Set $\tau_0=\frac{1+i}{2}$ and $\tau_1=\frac{1-i}{2}$; here $\tau$ names these coefficients, not the exponent of the address-interchange page. Then $\tau_0^2+\tau_1^2=0$ and $2\tau_0\tau_1=1$. For a binary vector $v$ (the letter $v$ names vectors here, not the vertices of other pages), $X_v$ is the translation by $v$ on functions of addresses. <cite data-source="paper">Here (Xv f )(x) = f (x + v).</cite> Call $K_v=\tau_0 I+\tau_1 X_v$ the two-point kernel for $v$. Since $X_v^2=I$, its inverse is $\tau_1 I+\tau_0 X_v$, because $(\tau_0 I+\tau_1X_v)(\tau_1 I+\tau_0X_v)=2\tau_0\tau_1 I+(\tau_0^2+\tau_1^2)X_v=I$.

An edge runs from a tail label $U$ to a head label $V$, and its frame change is $C_VC_U^{-1}$. The orthogonal residual $\Delta$ of an edge is the orthogonal complement of the smaller label inside the larger one. On an increasing edge $V=U\perp\Delta$, and on a decreasing edge $U=V\perp\Delta$. Let $\mathcal O$ be an orthonormal basis of $\Delta$, a set of vectors $v$ with $v\cdot v=1$ and $v\cdot v'=0$ for distinct $v,v'\in\mathcal O$. <cite data-source="paper">For the binary label space, every nonzero orthogonal residual of an edge has an orthonormal basis.</cite>

Take an increasing edge and an address $x$. The vector $P_\Delta x=\sum_{v\in\mathcal O}(v\cdot x)\,v$ lies in $\Delta$, and $x-P_\Delta x$ is orthogonal to every $v\in\mathcal O$, so it lies in $\Delta^\perp$. Then $P_Vx=P_Ux+P_\Delta x$: the right side lies in $V$, and $x-P_Ux-P_\Delta x$ is orthogonal to $U$ (since $x-P_Ux\in U^\perp$ and $P_\Delta x\in\Delta\subseteq U^\perp$) and to $\Delta$ (since $x-P_\Delta x\in\Delta^\perp$ and $P_Ux\in U\subseteq\Delta^\perp$), so it lies in $V^\perp$.

For binary vectors $z,w$ (letters local to this argument), $\mathrm{wt}(z+w)=\mathrm{wt}(z)+\mathrm{wt}(w)-2|\mathrm{supp}(z)\cap\mathrm{supp}(w)|$, because a coordinate in both supports counts twice in the first sum and not at all in the second. <cite data-source="paper">If z · w = 0, the intersection cardinality is even, so weight is additive modulo four.</cite> Now $P_Ux\cdot P_\Delta x=0$, since $P_\Delta x\in\Delta\subseteq U^\perp$, so $\mathrm{wt}(P_Vx)\equiv\mathrm{wt}(P_Ux)+\mathrm{wt}(P_\Delta x)\pmod 4$. The summands $(v\cdot x)\,v$ are pairwise orthogonal, and each partial sum lies in the span of the earlier basis vectors, which is orthogonal to the next summand. Applying the same fact one summand at a time gives $\mathrm{wt}(P_\Delta x)\equiv\sum_{v\in\mathcal O}[v\cdot x]\,\mathrm{wt}(v)\pmod 4$. Since $q_V(x)$ and $q_U(x)$ are these weights modulo 4,
$$q_V(x)-q_U(x)\equiv\sum_{v\in\mathcal O}\mathrm{wt}(v)\,[v\cdot x]\pmod 4$$
on an increasing edge, and the right side is negated on a decreasing edge, where the roles of $U$ and $V$ swap. <cite data-source="paper">Each v has norm one, so wt(v) is odd.</cite>

Let $\varepsilon_v\in\{1,-1\}$ with $\varepsilon_v\equiv\mathrm{wt}(v)\pmod 4$ on an increasing edge and $\varepsilon_v\equiv-\mathrm{wt}(v)\pmod 4$ on a decreasing edge. Since $\mathrm{wt}(v)$ is odd, such $\varepsilon_v$ exists, and $i^{q_V(x)-q_U(x)}=\prod_{v\in\mathcal O}i^{\varepsilon_v[v\cdot x]}$.

For $b\in\{0,1\}$ and $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$, $i^{\varepsilon b}=\frac{1+i\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{1-i\varepsilon}{2}(-1)^b$, since both sides equal $1$ at $b=0$ and $i\varepsilon$ at $b=1$. With $b=[v\cdot x]$, $\mathrm{diag}_x\big(i^{\varepsilon_v[v\cdot x]}\big)=\frac{1+i\varepsilon_v}{2}I+\frac{1-i\varepsilon_v}{2}\,\mathrm{diag}_x\big((-1)^{[v\cdot x]}\big)$. The identity $H\,\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)\,H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, <cite data-source="paper">tensored over the coordinates in supp(v)</cite> turns the sign $(-1)^{[v\cdot x]}$, a product of one-coordinate signs over $\mathrm{supp}(v)$, into $X_v$. So conjugating by $H^{\otimes m}$ gives $\frac{1+i\varepsilon_v}{2}I+\frac{1-i\varepsilon_v}{2}X_v$. For $\varepsilon_v=1$ this is $K_v$, and for $\varepsilon_v=-1$ it is $\tau_1 I+\tau_0X_v=K_v^{-1}$.

Since $(H^{\otimes m})^2=I$ and diagonal operators multiply pointwise, $C_VC_U^{-1}=H^{\otimes m}\,\mathrm{diag}_x\big(i^{q_V(x)-q_U(x)}\big)\,H^{\otimes m}=\prod_{v\in\mathcal O}K_v^{\varepsilon_v}$. Each edge therefore costs one two-point kernel per basis vector. The paper chooses its intermediate labels for this reason, <cite data-source="paper">to make the changes of frame inexpensive</cite>.

For $m=1$, take the labels $\{0\}$ and $\mathbb F_2$. Write $C=C_{\mathbb F_2}=H\,\mathrm{diag}(1,i)\,H$, which equals $\tau_0I+\tau_1X_1$, a single kernel; $C_{\{0\}}=I$ since $q_{\{0\}}=0$. The formula reproduces this: the edge is increasing with $\mathcal O=\{1\}$ and $\varepsilon_1=1$, so $C_{\mathbb F_2}C_{\{0\}}^{-1}=K_1$.

For general $m$, take the route $U_0=\{0\}$ and $U_\delta=\mathrm{span}(e_1,\dots,e_\delta)$ for $1\le\delta\le m$, where $e_\delta$ is the standard basis vector with a single $1$ at position $\delta$. Each $U_\delta$ is nondegenerate, because the Gram matrix of $e_1,\dots,e_\delta$ is the identity. The edge from $U_{\delta-1}$ to $U_\delta$ is increasing with $\mathcal O=\{e_\delta\}$ and $\varepsilon_{e_\delta}=1$, so its exponent difference is $x_\delta$, the $\delta$-th coordinate of $x$. The intermediate frames cancel, so
$$C_{U_m}C_{U_0}^{-1}=H^{\otimes m}\,\mathrm{diag}_x\big(i^{\mathrm{wt}(x)}\big)\,H^{\otimes m},$$
because the exponents telescope to $q_{\mathbb F_2^m}(x)-q_{\{0\}}(x)=\mathrm{wt}(x)$, with $P_{\mathbb F_2^m}$ the identity. Since $i^{\mathrm{wt}(x)}=\prod_\delta i^{x_\delta}$, the diagonal is the $m$-fold tensor power of $\mathrm{diag}(1,i)$, and the mixed-product rule gives $C_{U_m}C_{U_0}^{-1}=(H\,\mathrm{diag}(1,i)\,H)^{\otimes m}=C^{\otimes m}$.

The paper's version applies <cite data-source="paper">After source and sink diagonal corrections, and undoing the signed bank exchange</cite> before it telescopes, and concludes that <cite data-source="paper">phase frames gives C ⊗m on every role</cite>. Those corrections are outside this page; the computation above is the telescoping step along the route $U_\delta$.
