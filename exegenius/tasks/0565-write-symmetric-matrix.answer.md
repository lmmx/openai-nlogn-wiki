A symmetric matrix is a special case of the matrices in [Linear algebra basics](linear-algebra-basics.md): a square matrix equal to its own transpose. It is the property behind the side wires of the [Subset-intersection network](subset-intersection-network.md), which join pairs of neighbouring triples in a way that does not depend on the order of the pair. <cite data-source="paper">A side wire still joins neighboring physical triples in stage 2 because the neighbor relation is</cite> symmetric.

Let $A$ be an $m\times m$ matrix with entries in $F$. The entry of $A$ in row $i$ and column $j$ is written $A_{ij}$, an element of $F$, for $i,j$ from $1$ to $m$. The *transpose* $A^{\top}$ is the $m\times m$ matrix whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $A_{ji}$, so its rows are the columns of $A$. The matrix $A$ is *symmetric* when $A^{\top}=A$, that is, when $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$ for all $i$ and $j$.

Take $m=2$ over the real numbers. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric, because its entries in positions $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ are both $2$. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ is not, because its entry in row $1$, column $2$ is $2$ while its entry in row $2$, column $1$ is $0$. The diagonal entries are compared only with themselves, so they may take any values.

Let $S$ and $T$ be three-element subsets of a finite set. They are *neighbours* when they share exactly one element: <cite data-source="paper">S, T are neighbors when |S ∩ T | = 1.</cite> The table whose entry at the pair $(S,T)$ is $1$ when $S$ and $T$ are neighbours, and $0$ otherwise, is symmetric. Indeed $|S\cap T|=|T\cap S|$, so $S$ and $T$ are neighbours exactly when $T$ and $S$ are, and the entry at $(S,T)$ equals the entry at $(T,S)$.
