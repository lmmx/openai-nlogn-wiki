## This page

Concept: Chinese-remainder map to prime cyclic axes
Gloss: An algebra isomorphism sending one cyclic convolution of length S = s_1⋯s_d (pairwise coprime) to a convolution on a product of d cyclic axes, implemented by triangular controlled cyclic shifts.

## This page in the argument

The step: The radix-digit coefficient list can be placed on a product of $d$ distinct prime cyclic axes by an isomorphism $\Phi$ that preserves cyclic convolution and is realized by triangular controlled shifts.
How: Use the Chinese-remainder isomorphism $x\mapsto\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i}$, compute each axis coordinate from lower ones by a formula that is triangular in tape order, and implement the updates as controlled cyclic shifts with axis moves.
Why: A convolution on a long cyclic axis of composite length cannot be tensored, but one on a product of coprime cyclic axes can be tensored axis by axis. (the sources say so: "To use the source tensor, we first place the ordinary coefficient stream" (paper))

Its sub-steps, in order:

- The map $\Phi:x\mapsto\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i}$ is an algebra isomorphism that preserves cyclic convolution.
- Axis coordinates are $b_i=a_i+\mu_i\sum_{j<i}a_jP_j \pmod{s_i}$, triangular in tape order, so each axis is one prefix-controlled cyclic shift.
- $\Phi$, its inverse and the padding operations cost $O(Tp\,d^2(1+\ell^\tau))$.

It serves these steps, from the main result down:

- A single deterministic fixed-tape Turing machine multiplies two $n$-bit integers exactly in $O(n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa})$ worst-case time with $\kappa=2^{-182}$.; how: Start from the Gaussian-resampling reduction of integer multiplication to convolution; replace the two costly operations of the synthetic transforms (address rearrangement and butterfly layers) by faster procedures built from one recursive linear-network exchange, assemble the exact algorithm, and account for its cost. (on [Multiplying n-bit integers in O(n (lg n)^(1−κ)) time](main-theorem.md))
- The multiplication algorithm, run on $x\#y$, outputs exactly the $2n$-bit product of the two $n$-bit integers.; how: Split the inputs into radix digits, map them by $\Phi$ to prime axes, take two source transforms, multiply pointwise, apply the opposite-sign transform with two rescalings by $S$, round, undo $\Phi$ and propagate carries. (on [The multiplication algorithm](multiplication-algorithm.md))

**S7.2.1**: The map $\Phi:x\mapsto\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i}$ is an algebra isomorphism that preserves cyclic convolution.
Source: "$\Phi$ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic convolution." (paper)
> Let $s_1,\ldots,s_d$ be the pairwise coprime source lengths, let $S=\prod_i s_i$, and put \[ P_i=\prod_{j<i}s_j,\qquad \mu_i=P_i^{-1}\pmod{s_i}. \] Here $P_i$ denotes an integer prefix product, not a Fourier-coordinate permutation. The assignment \[ \Phi:\mathbb C[x]/(x^S-1)\longrightarrow \mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_d]/(x_1^{s_1}-1,\ldots,x_d^{s_d}-1), \qquad x\longmapsto\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i} \] respects $x^S=1$, so it defines an algebra map. The target monomial group has size $S$, and the image of $x$ has order $S$, because each $\mu_i$ is a unit and the $s_i$ are pairwise coprime. Its powers therefore run through all target basis monomials exactly once. Hence $\Phi$ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic convolution.

**S7.2.2**: Axis coordinates are $b_i=a_i+\mu_i\sum_{j<i}a_jP_j \pmod{s_i}$, triangular in tape order, so each axis is one prefix-controlled cyclic shift.
Source: "b_i=a_i+\mu_i\sum_{j<i}a_jP_j\pmod{s_i}." (paper)
> Every ordinary coefficient index has a unique mixed-radix expression \[ k=\sum_{j=1}^d a_jP_j,\qquad 0\le a_j<s_j. \] The coordinate of $\Phi(x^k)$ in axis $i$ is \begin{equation}\label{eq:triangular-crt} b_i=a_i+\mu_i\sum_{j<i}a_jP_j\pmod{s_i}. \end{equation} Indeed terms with $j>i$ are divisible by $s_i$, and $\mu_iP_i=1$ modulo $s_i$. Numerical order of $k$ is lexicographic order of $(a_d,\ldots,a_1)$. Insert the padding in one nested scan of the full box $\prod_i[0,t_i)$ in this lexicographic order, with $a_d$ the outermost counter and $a_1$ the innermost. At an address satisfying $a_i<s_i$ for every $i$, copy the next original coefficient record; at every other address, write a zero record. The valid addresses occur in the original coefficient order, so each record is copied to its intended mixed-radix address. To delete the padding after the inverse axis reorder, use the same enumeration, copying the record at each valid address and skipping the record at each invalid address.

**S7.2.3**: $\Phi$, its inverse and the padding operations cost $O(Tp\,d^2(1+\ell^\tau))$.
Source: "their padding operations cost" (paper)
> The axis widths are $\ell$ or $\ell-1$. To exchange two adjacent axes of unequal width, move the longer axis's leading bit outside their equal-width pair, swap that pair by the proved chunk-swap procedure, and put the leading bit at its new position. This adds a fixed number of linear passes to the equal-width swap cost. Reversing all axes uses $O(d^2)$ such moves. Therefore the map $\Phi$, its inverse, and their padding operations cost \[ O\bigl(Tp\,d^2(1+\ell^\tau)\bigr). \] This construction describes payload movement from address $a$ to its new address. It is distinct from the operator convention $(R_cz)_j=z_{cj}$ used for Fourier dilations, which moves a payload from address $j$ to $c^{-1}j$.
