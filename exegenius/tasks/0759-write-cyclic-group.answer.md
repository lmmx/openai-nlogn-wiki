A cyclic group is a group whose elements are all the powers of one fixed element, its generator. It is the algebra behind the cyclic axes of the proof: the first reduction places the radix-digit coefficients on <cite data-source="paper">prime cyclic axes by a Chinese-remainder map that preserves convolution.</cite>

A *group* is a set with an associative operation, an identity element $1$, and an inverse for each element. Write the operation as multiplication. Let $\upsilon$ be a fixed element of a group, its *generator*. For an integer $\vartheta$, $\upsilon^\vartheta$ is the product of $\vartheta$ copies of $\upsilon$, with $\upsilon^{-1}$ the inverse of $\upsilon$ and $\upsilon^0=1$. The set $\langle\upsilon\rangle=\{\upsilon^\vartheta:\vartheta\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ of these powers is the *cyclic group* generated by $\upsilon$. The *order* of $\upsilon$ is the least positive integer $\vartheta$ with $\upsilon^\vartheta=1$, written $\operatorname{ord}(\upsilon)$, the number of distinct powers of $\upsilon$ when that is finite.

Take a cyclic group of order $6$ generated by $\upsilon$, so that $\upsilon^\vartheta=\upsilon^{\vartheta \bmod 6}$. The generator reaches all six elements $\upsilon^0,\dots,\upsilon^5$. The power $\upsilon^2$ does not: its powers are $\upsilon^0$, $\upsilon^2$, $\upsilon^4$, and then $\upsilon^6=\upsilon^0$ repeats, so $\operatorname{ord}(\upsilon^2)=3$.

The cyclic group used by the proof is built from a pairwise coprime family. The cyclic group here is based on [Coprime](coprime.md) integers: $\langle\upsilon\rangle$ has order $W$, the product of the pairwise coprime family $f_1,\dots,f_J$. Each $\mu_\sigma$ is the integer of the Coprime page, a unit modulo $f_\sigma$, because $W_\sigma\mu_\sigma\equiv 1 \pmod{f_\sigma}$ there. Let $\mu=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_J)$ in the transform group $G$. Let $\Gamma$ send $\upsilon$ to $\mu$ and $\upsilon^\vartheta$ to $\vartheta\mu$, the tuple whose $\sigma$-th entry is $\vartheta\mu_\sigma$ mod $f_\sigma$. This is well defined because $\upsilon^W=1$ and $W\mu=0$.

The paper's argument rests on the claim that the image of the generator <cite data-source="paper">has order S, because each µi is a unit and the si are pairwise coprime.</cite> Here $\Gamma(\upsilon)=\mu$ has order $W$, which plays the role of the paper's $S$. Indeed, $\vartheta\mu=0$ means $f_\sigma \mid \vartheta\mu_\sigma$ for every $\sigma$. Since $\mu_\sigma$ is a unit modulo $f_\sigma$, this gives $f_\sigma\mid\vartheta$ for every $\sigma$, and since the $f_\sigma$ are pairwise coprime, $W\mid\vartheta$. Conversely $W\mu=0$.

So the $W$ values $\vartheta\mu$ for $\vartheta=0,\dots,W-1$ are distinct: if $\vartheta\mu=\vartheta'\mu$ then $W\mid\vartheta-\vartheta'$. They are $W$ elements of $G$, which has $W$ elements, so $\Gamma$ is one-to-one and onto. It is also a homomorphism, meaning it preserves products: $\Gamma(\upsilon^\vartheta\upsilon^{\vartheta'})=(\vartheta+\vartheta')\mu=\Gamma(\upsilon^\vartheta)+\Gamma(\upsilon^{\vartheta'})$. So $\Gamma$ is a bijective homomorphism.

For sequences $a$ and $b$ indexed by the elements of a group, their *cyclic convolution* $a\ast b$ has value at each element $g$ equal to the sum of $a(h)\,b(k)$ over all pairs $h,k$ with $hk=g$. A bijective homomorphism sends the pairs with $hk=g$ exactly onto the pairs $(x,y)$ in $G$ with $x+y=\Gamma(g)$, and it sends $a$ and $b$ to sequences on $G$ whose convolution is the same sum over $G$. So transporting sequences along $\Gamma$ turns cyclic convolution on $\langle\upsilon\rangle$ into cyclic convolution on $G$. Hence $\Gamma$ preserves cyclic convolution.
