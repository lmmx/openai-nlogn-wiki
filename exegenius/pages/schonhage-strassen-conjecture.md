The Schönhage–Strassen optimality conjecture is a claim about the fastest possible multiplication of two integers on a multitape Turing machine. It is the yardstick against which the target result is compared.

Here $n$ is the number of bits in each of the two integers being multiplied. The time is the worst-case number of steps over inputs of that length. The conjecture says that $n\log n$ is the best possible order of growth for it, where $\log$ denotes the same base-2 logarithm as $\lg$.

Schönhage and Strassen's 1971 algorithm multiplies two $n$-bit integers on a multitape Turing machine in $O(n\log n\log\log n)$ time <cite data-source="paper">Sch\"onhage and Strassen proved in 1971 that two $n$-bit integers can be multiplied in $O(n\log n\log\log n)$ time on a multitape Turing machine</cite>. They proposed $n\log n$ as the optimal order of growth <cite data-source="paper">proposed $n\log n$ as the optimal order of growth</cite>.

Harvey and van der Hoeven later gave an unconditional $O(n\log n)$ upper bound <cite data-source="paper">Harvey and van der Hoeven reached the unconditional $O(n\log n)$ upper bound</cite> for the same problem. Since the conjecture concerns multitape machines, this gives an $O(n\log n)$ multitape algorithm. The open part of the conjecture is therefore the matching lower bound: every multitape machine needs $\Omega(n\log n)$ worst-case time to multiply $n$-bit integers.

The conjecture is posed for multitape machines and is contrasted with the [fixed-tape machine model](fixed-tape-model.md), in which the target result is measured. The target result, [Fixed-tape integer multiplication with a power saving](fixed-tape-multiplication.md), computes the integer product exactly on one fixed-tape machine. Its worst-case time is $O(n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa})$ for a fixed constant $\kappa>0$, and its exponent $1-\kappa$ on $\lg n$ is less than 1. The target result obtains a strict power saving in the logarithmic factor <cite data-source="paper">We obtain a strict power saving in the logarithmic factor</cite>, which is why it is compared with the conjecture.
