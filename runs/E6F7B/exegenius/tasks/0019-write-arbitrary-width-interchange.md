## This page

Concept: Address-chunk interchange in time O(V u^τ)
Gloss: Exchanging two disjoint contiguous address fields of u bits (any width, any gaps, even one-bit payload) in O(V u^τ) for a fixed τ = 1 − 2^{−50} < 1, by peeling off high digits to make rows, padding, and splitting the width in base m; the intermediate steps are XOR combinations though the result is an exact permutation.
Heading: Address-chunk interchange in time $O(Vu^\tau)$

The symbols in this page's heading denote (say so on the page):

- $V$ (stored bit volume): The number of data bits stored in the array being rearranged.
- $u$ (chunk width): The number of bits in each of the two interchanged address chunks.
- $\tau$ (interchange exponent): A fixed constant strictly below one (1 − 2^{−50}) bounding the growth of the cost with the chunk width.

## This page in the argument

The step: Two disjoint contiguous address fields of $u$ bits, of any width and with any gaps, are interchanged in $O(Vu^\tau)$ steps for a fixed $\tau=1-2^{-50}<1$.
How: Handle widths that are powers of $m$ with enough rows by the recursive network, then reach arbitrary widths by peeling off high digits to supply rows, padding the row count, splitting the width in base $m$ and padding the field ranges to a radix.
Why: The recursion needs a row count divisible by a power of $W$ and a width that is a power of $m$; both can be arranged cheaply. (the sources say so: "Rows for a general array can be supplied by a few of its own high digits." (paper))
Striking feature: The final effect is an exact permutation of the data, although the intermediate steps form XOR combinations of data streams; this is why the cost can fall below a general sort of addresses. (explain why it holds, from the method)

Its sub-steps, in order:

- For a rational matrix $A$ of rank $a$, the shear $(H,D)\mapsto(H+AD,D)$ over $\mathbb Z/q^b\mathbb Z$ takes exactly $a$ interchanges of $b$-digit fields and $O(V)$ other work. (on [Cost of a rational matrix shear](matrix-shear-cost.md))
- For $e=m^k$, two disjoint address chunks of $e$ digits, with enough preceding rows, are interchanged in time $O(Ve^\tau)$. (on [Recursive power-width address interchange](power-width-interchange.md))
- Peeling off the top $\rho$ digits of each chunk, interchanging them and joining them in front gives a row field of range at least $W^k$ before both remaining chunks.
- Padding the row count to a multiple of $W^k$ with zero rows and splitting the remaining width in base $m$ into pieces of width $m^j$ gives total cost $O(Ve^\tau)$ by a geometric sum.
- Padding binary ranges $[2^u]$ to radix-$q$ ranges $[q^e]$ costs only a fixed factor and, since $e=O(u)$, gives the bound $O(Vu^\tau)$.

It serves these steps, from the main result down:

- One deterministic fixed-tape Turing machine multiplies two $n$-bit integers exactly in time $O(n(\lg n)^{1-\kappa})$ with $\kappa=2^{-182}$.; how: Keep the transform-based multiplication of Harvey and van der Hoeven (Gaussian resampling, Bluestein chirp, synthetic polynomial transforms) but replace its two costly tape operations, address rearrangement and butterfly layers, by faster procedures obtained from rank-saving linear networks, then check exactness and total cost. (on [Integer multiplication in O(n (lg n)^(1−κ)) time](main-theorem.md))
- Two tape procedures cost $O(Vu^\tau)$ for interchanging two address fields of $u$ bits and $O(Vd^{\lambda'})$ for a butterfly layer on at most $d$ chunks, where $V$ is the stored bit volume, $\tau<1$ and $\lambda'<1$.; how: Derive both procedures from the same device: a fixed linear network whose edge changes are cheaper smaller calls of the same procedure, on top of elementary linear-time stream operations.

**S1.4.3**: Peeling off the top $\rho$ digits of each chunk, interchanging them and joining them in front gives a row field of range at least $W^k$ before both remaining chunks.
Source: "Write each chunk as its top $\rho$ digits followed by its remaining" (paper)
> Write each chunk as its top $\rho$ digits followed by its remaining digits, denoted $h^+,h^-$ and $d^+,d^-$, respectively. Interchange the corresponding high digits one pair at a time. The high positions now contain $d^+$ and $h^+$; move the digits of the latter, preserving their order, into consecutive positions immediately after $d^+$. Suppressing the prefix and suffix, the field orders during the construction are \[ \begin{aligned} &(h^+,h^-,g,d^+,d^-),\\ &(d^+,h^+,h^-,g,d^-) &&\text{after exchanging and joining the high parts},\\ &(d^+,h^+,d^-,g,h^-) &&\text{after interchanging the remaining parts},\\ &(d^+,d^-,g,h^+,h^-) &&\text{after separating the high parts again}. \end{aligned} \] In the two middle orders, the first two fields form a row field of range \[ R=q^{2\rho}\ge W^k \] before both remaining chunks. The joining moves preserve the order of the remaining digits and the gap field. Exchanging and joining the high parts costs $O(V\rho)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:elementary-streams}. Append zero rows, separately within each preceding prefix, until the row count is \[ R'=W^k\left\lceil R/W^k\right\rceil<2R. \] This changes the row field's length; it need not remain a power of $q$ during the computation. The padded logical volume is less than $2V$.

**S1.4.5**: Padding binary ranges $[2^u]$ to radix-$q$ ranges $[q^e]$ costs only a fixed factor and, since $e=O(u)$, gives the bound $O(Vu^\tau)$.
Source: "Finally, for binary chunks let $e$ be the least integer with" (paper)
> Finally, for binary chunks let $e$ be the least integer with $q^e\ge2^u$. Pad each of the two chunk ranges numerically from $[2^u]$ to $[q^e]$, putting zero in an entry if either chunk coordinate is invalid. The increase in volume is less than $q^2$, a fixed factor. The padding is a nested scan through consecutive valid and invalid intervals, with their lengths prepared from the shape. It costs $O(V)$ with a constant depending on $q$. Lexicographic radix-$q$ order of $[q^e]$ is its numerical order, just as binary order of $[2^u]$ is its numerical order. Padding therefore preserves the order of the existing entries without converting the digits of every address. The completed interchange preserves the set on which both chunk coordinates lie below $2^u$. Scan away the other entries to recover the desired binary array. Since $e=O(u)$ for fixed $q$, the bound is $O(Vu^\tau)$. \end{proof}

## Evidence from the sources, with the paragraph around each quote

- "(p,h,g,d,z)\longmapsto(p,d,g,h,z)" (paper)

  > \begin{lemma}[Arbitrary-width interchange] \label{lem:chunk-swap} There exist fixed $0<\tau<1$ and one fixed finite-alphabet multitape procedure with the following property. For positive integers $P,G,B$ and $u\ge1$, it transforms an arbitrary bit array on \[ [P]\times[2^u]\times[G]\times[2^u]\times[B] \] by the address permutation \[ (p,h,g,d,z)\longmapsto(p,d,g,h,z) \] in $O(Vu^\tau)$ steps, where $V=PG B\,2^{2u}$. The integers specifying the shape are part of the input. The time includes their processing, all padding and unpadding, and fixed-tape workspace cleanup. In particular, $B=1$ is allowed. \end{lemma}

- "The first procedure interchanges two disjoint contiguous address fields" (paper)

  > The first procedure interchanges two disjoint contiguous address fields of $u$ bits in an array whose addresses form a complete Cartesian product. Its cost is $O(Vu^\tau)$ for a fixed $\tau<1$. Although its final effect is an exact permutation, its intermediate steps form pointwise XOR combinations of data. The final permutation is exact for arbitrary initial auxiliary values.
