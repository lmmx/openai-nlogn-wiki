The Gram matrix of a list of vectors is the table of values that a [Bilinear form](bilinear-form.md) takes on every pair of vectors in the list. The paper uses it to check that the subspace labels of its construction are nondegenerate, and those labels fix the count of smaller calls that the [Rank-saving recursion](rank-saving-recursion.md) must keep below $Wm$.

The Gram matrix is based on the [Bilinear form](bilinear-form.md), since each of its entries is the value of that form on a pair of vectors. Let $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ be a bilinear form on a vector space over a field, that is, a map of two vectors to a scalar that is linear in each argument when the other is fixed. Let $\sigma$ be the number of vectors in a list, and write $\mathbf v_i$ for the $i$th vector of the list, so $i$ runs from $1$ to $\sigma$. The Gram matrix $\mathcal G$ is the $\sigma\times\sigma$ matrix whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $\mathcal G_{ij}=\langle \mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_j\rangle$. Here $i$ and $j$ index the list, so these letters change meaning from their earlier uses as an imaginary unit and as a coordinate index. The Gram matrix is a matrix of the kind defined in [Linear algebra basics](linear-algebra-basics.md): a square array with rows and columns indexed by the vectors of the list.

Take $\mathbb F_2^3$, the triples of bits, with the ordinary dot product computed in $\mathbb F_2$, where $1+1=0$. For the two vectors $\mathbf v_1=(1,1,0)$ and $\mathbf v_2=(0,1,1)$ the values are
$$\langle\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_1\rangle=1+1+0=0,\qquad \langle\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\rangle=0+1+0=1,\qquad \langle\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_2\rangle=0+1+1=0,$$
so $\sigma=2$ and $\mathcal G=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

The matrix decides nondegeneracy. A subspace is nondegenerate when no nonzero vector of it is orthogonal to every vector of it, where two vectors are orthogonal when the form is zero on them. Let $\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_\sigma$ be linearly independent, and let $\mathbf x=c_1\mathbf v_1+\dots+c_\sigma\mathbf v_\sigma$ be a vector in their span, where $c_j$ is the scalar coefficient of $\mathbf v_j$. For a symmetric form such as the dot product, bilinearity gives $\langle \mathbf v_i,\mathbf x\rangle=\sum_j \mathcal G_{ij}c_j$. So $\mathbf x$ is orthogonal to the whole span exactly when $\mathcal G$ times the column of coefficients is zero. Since the vectors are independent, $\mathbf x\ne 0$ exactly when the coefficients are not all zero. Hence the span is nondegenerate exactly when $\mathcal G$ is invertible. In the example, $\det\mathcal G=0\cdot 0-1\cdot 1=1$ in $\mathbb F_2$, which is nonzero, so the two vectors span a nondegenerate plane.

The paper applies this test to indicator vectors of triples. <cite data-source="paper">For a triple T let tT be its indicator vector</cite>, and its values under the form are the entries of a Gram matrix of those vectors. The paper requires that <cite data-source="paper">A label is a nondegenerate subspace of a fixed bilinear</cite> space, and it concludes from the values that <cite data-source="paper">Thus every triple line is nondegenerate, and neighboring triple lines are orthogonal</cite>. For the complex network the diagonal values are the same kind of quantity: <cite data-source="paper">here tT · tT = 1.</cite>

The labels matter for the count because <cite data-source="paper">Their dimension changes count the smaller operations needed on an edge</cite>, and the saving depends on keeping that total below $Wm$. <cite data-source="paper">This strict inequality is the source of the power saving.</cite>
