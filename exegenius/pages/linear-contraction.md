A linear contraction is a linear map defined on the vector spaces of [Linear algebra basics](linear-algebra-basics.md) that never increases the largest coordinate modulus of a vector. It keeps errors from growing through a computation, as exact recovery needs ([Exact coefficient recovery](recovery-and-error.md)).

Let $T\colon \mathbb{C}^m \to \mathbb{C}^{m'}$ be a linear map, where $m$ is the number of coordinates of its input vectors and $m'$ is the number of coordinates of its output vectors. For $x \in \mathbb{C}^m$, the norm $\|x\|$ is the largest modulus $|x_j|$ over its coordinates $x_j$, where $|z|$ is the modulus of a complex number $z$. <cite data-source="paper">The vector norm is the maximum modulus</cite>

$T$ is a linear contraction when $\|Tx\| \le \|x\|$ for every $x \in \mathbb{C}^m$. Its operator norm $\|T\|$ is defined through the [Supremum](supremum.md) of $\|Tx\|$ over all $x$ with $\|x\| \le 1$. So $T$ is a contraction exactly when $\|T\| \le 1$. <cite data-source="paper">All operator norms are induced by this norm</cite>

A computation holds $x+\xi$ in place of the exact input $x$, where $\xi$ is the carried error. A step computes $T(x+\xi)+\zeta$, where $\zeta$ is the rounding error that the step introduces. The output error is $T\xi+\zeta$, and $\|T\xi+\zeta\| \le \|\xi\|+\|\zeta\|$, because $\|T\xi\| \le \|\xi\|$ and $|a+b| \le |a|+|b|$ for each coordinate. <cite data-source="paper">A linear contraction propagates an input error without increasing it.</cite> <cite data-source="paper">Composing such algorithms adds their errors: at each step, insert the exact map at the computed</cite> The paper's <cite data-source="paper">the recovered coefficient error is strictly below 1/2</cite>, so rounding returns the exact integer coefficients.

The normalized transform $F_q$ of the [Discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) sends $f$ to the vector with coordinates $(F_q f)_\ell = \frac{1}{q}\sum_{s=0}^{q-1} \omega^{s\ell} f_s$. Each $|\omega^{s\ell}| = 1$, so $|(F_q f)_\ell| \le \frac{1}{q}\sum_{s} |f_s| \le \|f\|$. <cite data-source="paper">normalized Fourier transform above is a contraction, since each output averages inputs multiplied</cite>

For example, take $q = 2$, so $\omega = -1$, and $f = (1, i)$. Then $(F_2 f)_0 = \frac{1+i}{2}$ and $(F_2 f)_1 = \frac{1-i}{2}$ have modulus $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.71 \le 1 = \|f\|$.
