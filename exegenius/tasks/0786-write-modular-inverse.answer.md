A modular inverse of an integer $h$, the integer to be inverted, modulo a positive integer $p$, the modulus, is an integer that undoes multiplication by $h$ up to multiples of $p$. The paper's [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) uses such inverses as exponents, and its claim about the order of the map's image depends on them.

An integer $\mu$ is a modular inverse of $h$ modulo $p$ when $h\mu \equiv 1 \pmod p$. A modular inverse is defined by a [congruence](congruence-modulo.md) modulo $p$: $h\mu$ and $1$ leave the same remainder on division by $p$, so $h\mu - 1$ is a multiple of $p$.

For $p = 5$ and $h = 3$, the integer $\mu = 2$ is an inverse, since $3 \cdot 2 - 1 = 5$. For $p = 4$ and $h = 2$ there is no inverse: $2\mu$ is even for every integer $\mu$, so $2\mu - 1$ is odd and cannot be a multiple of $4$.

An inverse exists exactly when $h$ and $p$ are [coprime](coprime.md), that is, when their greatest common divisor is $1$. If $h\mu \equiv 1 \pmod p$, then $1$ is $h\mu$ minus a multiple of $p$, so every common divisor of $h$ and $p$ divides $1$. Conversely, the inverse is obtained from [Bézout's identity](bezout-identity.md) applied to $h$ and $p$. When the greatest common divisor is $1$, the identity gives an integer $\mu$ and a second integer $\nu$, the cofactor that multiplies $p$, with $h\mu + p\nu = 1$. Reducing this equation modulo $p$ gives $h\mu \equiv 1 \pmod p$, so $\mu$ is an inverse. For $h = 3$ and $p = 5$, $3 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot (-1) = 1$, so $\mu = 2$ and $\nu = -1$.

The paper's exponents are inverses of this kind. For each cyclic axis, the exponent is the inverse, modulo that axis's length, of the product of the lengths of the axes before it. Each axis length is the number of values its coordinate takes, and the lengths are pairwise coprime, so each such product is coprime to the length it is inverted modulo. Each inverse supplies exponents for placing the [radix-digit coefficients](radix-digit-convolution.md) on cyclic axes.

Let $S$ be the product of all axis lengths, the number of elements in the cyclic group that the map targets. The order of an element is its least positive power equal to $1$. Together the inverses help determine the generator of a [cyclic group](cyclic-group.md) of order $S$. The paper states that the image of its generator <cite data-source="paper">has order S, because each µi is a unit and the si are pairwise coprime</cite>. Here a unit is an integer with an inverse modulo its axis length, so the cited clause says that every exponent is invertible. The existence of each inverse, shown above, is what the clause relies on.
