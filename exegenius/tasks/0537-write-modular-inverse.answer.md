Let $\beta$ be the integer to be inverted and $M \ge 1$ the modulus; the letter $M$ changes meaning here, from the machine of earlier pages to a modulus. A modular inverse of $\beta$ modulo $M$ is an integer $\mu$ with $\beta\mu$ [congruent](congruence-modulo.md) to $1$ modulo $M$, and the argument needs such inverses for the exponents of its [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md). Each inverse gives the exponents for placing the [radix-digit coefficients](radix-digit-convolution.md) on cyclic axes whose lengths are pairwise [coprime](coprime.md).

The inverse is defined by a [congruence](congruence-modulo.md) modulo $M$. An inverse exists exactly when $\beta$ and $M$ are [coprime](coprime.md), that is, when their greatest common divisor is $1$. If $\beta\mu \equiv 1 \pmod M$, then $\beta\mu - 1 = \sigma M$ for $\sigma$, the integer quotient $(\beta\mu-1)/M$, so every common divisor of $\beta$ and $M$ divides $1$. Conversely, the inverse is obtained from [Bézout's identity](bezout-identity.md) applied to $\beta$ and $-M$: it gives integers $\mu$ and $\sigma$ with $\beta\mu - \sigma M = 1$, and this $\mu$ is an inverse.

Let $d$ be the number of axes. For an axis index $i$ with $1 \le i \le d$ (here $i$ is not the imaginary unit), let $M_i$ be the length of axis $i$, the number of values its coordinate takes. The axis lengths are the paper's pairwise coprime source lengths: <cite data-source="paper">Let s1 , . . . , sd be the pairwise coprime source lengths</cite>, so $M_i = s_i$, and any two distinct axis lengths are coprime. Let $S = \prod_{i=1}^d M_i$. For each $i$, let $P_i = \prod_{j<i} M_j$ be the product of the lengths before axis $i$, where $j$ is an axis index with $1 \le j < i$; $P_1 = 1$ is the empty product. Each factor $M_j$ with $j<i$ is coprime to $M_i$, so $P_i$ is coprime to $M_i$. Let $\mu_i$ be the inverse of $P_i$ modulo $M_i$, which exists.

Each inverse $\mu_i$ computes exponents in a [cyclic group](cyclic-group.md) of order $S$. Let $\xi$ be a generator of that group, and for each axis $i$ let $\xi_i$ be a generator of a cyclic group of order $M_i$, so $\xi_i^{M_i} = 1$. The paper's map sends $\xi$ to $\prod_i \xi_i^{\mu_i}$, with $\xi_i$ playing the role of the paper's $x_i$. The order of an element is its least positive power equal to $1$. The paper asserts that this image has order $S$ (a unit is an integer with an inverse modulo $M_i$): <cite data-source="paper">because each µi is a unit and the si are pairwise coprime</cite>.

Here is the argument. Since each $M_i$ divides $S$, the image raised to the power $S$ is $1$. Conversely, let $\zeta$ be a positive integer with $\left(\prod_j \xi_j^{\mu_j}\right)^{\zeta} = 1$, and fix an axis $i$. Raising to the power $S/M_i$ turns every factor with $j \ne i$ into $1$, since $M_j$ divides $S/M_i$. This leaves $\xi_i^{(S/M_i)\zeta\mu_i} = 1$, so $M_i$ divides $(S/M_i)\zeta\mu_i$. The integers $S/M_i$ and $\mu_i$ are both coprime to $M_i$: the first is a product of lengths coprime to $M_i$, and $P_i\mu_i \equiv 1 \pmod{M_i}$ makes the second coprime. Hence $M_i$ divides $\zeta$. Doing this for every $i$, the pairwise coprime $M_i$ all divide $\zeta$, so their product $S$ divides $\zeta$. Thus the image has order $S$.

For $M_1 = 3$ and $M_2 = 5$, $P_1 = 1$ and $P_2 = 3$, so $\mu_1 = 1$ and $\mu_2 = 2$, since $3 \cdot 2 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$. The image is $\xi_1\xi_2^2$. Its $\zeta$-th power is $1$ exactly when $3 \mid \zeta$ and $5 \mid 2\zeta$, that is, when $15 \mid \zeta$, so the order is $15 = S$.
