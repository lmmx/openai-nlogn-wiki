Rank-saving recursion is the recursive scheme that gives the power saving in the logarithmic factor of [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md). Each call splits into several smaller calls, each on a stream holding a fixed fraction of the volume, and the number of smaller calls is strictly less than the wire count times the ambient dimension $m$. The cost per unit of data then grows by a factor strictly below $m$ at each level, and that gap is the saving.

## Counting the smaller calls

Each wire of the network has a fixed place in the network, called its role, and carries a label, a subspace of a fixed $m$-dimensional space. Labels at adjacent vertices are nested. Rank-saving recursion counts its smaller calls on the wires of the [subset-intersection network](subset-intersection-network.md). Let $W$ be the number of wires in the network, which is also the number of roles among which the rows of the array are distributed. To split an array, <cite data-source="paper">Distribute rows cyclically among the W roles</cite>, <cite data-source="paper">padding the row count for each prefix to a multiple of W</cite>, so every role stream holds the same volume $V/W$. Here $V$ is the stored volume of the array, as in [address-field interchange](address-interchange.md).

Let $s$ be the total number of smaller calls that one call makes; here $s$ changes the meaning it had on earlier pages. Each call splits into smaller calls to the [address-field interchange](address-interchange.md), each on one role stream of $V/W$ bits. <cite data-source="paper">A recursive call at parameter mf therefore uses s calls at parameter f</cite>. The parameter $\ell$ counts the selected axes of a [numerical layer](numerical-layer.md), or the digits of an address field for interchange.

An edge runs from a tail label $U$ to a head label $U'$. Its rank is <cite data-source="paper">the orthogonal complement of the smaller label inside the larger one</cite>, and since the labels are nested, its rank is the absolute change $|\Delta|$ in label dimension, where $\Delta=\dim U'-\dim U$. The rank counts the smaller calls on that edge: <cite data-source="paper">count the smaller operations needed on an edge</cite>. The total is the sum over all edges: <cite data-source="paper">The number of recursive calls is the sum of the edge ranks, exactly s.</cite>
Let $\mu$ be the sum of the dimensions of the source labels, and let $L$ be the total dimension lost along decreasing edges, those whose head label is smaller than their tail. Sources and sinks are the terminals of the network. <cite data-source="paper">The signed sum of dimension changes telescopes at every gate</cite>, because all incidences of one gate carry the same label. What remains is the terminals: <cite data-source="paper">the sum of sink dimensions is W m − N</cite>, so the signed sum of all $\Delta$ is $Wm-2\mu$. A decreasing edge satisfies $|\Delta|=\Delta+2\cdot(\text{loss})$, and an increasing edge satisfies $|\Delta|=\Delta$. So <cite data-source="paper">replacing signed changes by absolute changes adds 2L</cite>, and the total is $Wm-2\mu+2L$.

The two constructions differ by one source term. The complex construction gives $s=Wm-2\mu+2L$, as <cite data-source="paper">Wc m − 2N + 2Lc</cite> states. The bit construction has <cite data-source="paper">with an additional source contribution in the bit</cite>, giving $s=Wm-\mu+2L$, as <cite data-source="paper">Wb m − N + 2Lb</cite> states. Both are at most $Wm-\mu+2L$. The paper proves <cite data-source="paper">We will obtain L < N/2.</cite>, that is $2L<\mu$. Hence $s\le Wm-\mu+2L<Wm$, which is the key inequality: <cite data-source="paper">the total number s of smaller calls satisfies s < W m</cite>.

## Why this gives a power saving

Let $\gamma(\ell)$ be the cost of a call at parameter $\ell$ divided by its volume. A call at parameter $m\ell$ on volume $V$ makes $s$ calls at parameter $\ell$, each on volume $V/W$, plus linear work per level. Dividing by volume, <cite data-source="paper">recursive contribution by s/W < m.</cite> So, ignoring the linear work (which the approximation below absorbs), $\gamma(m\ell)\approx(s/W)\,\gamma(\ell)$.

Take $\ell=m^k$ and iterate: $\gamma(m^k)\approx(s/W)^k\gamma(1)$, where $\gamma(1)$ is a constant. Set $\tau=\log_m(s/W)$, the power-saving exponent. Then $(s/W)^k=m^{k\tau}=(m^k)^\tau$, so $\gamma(\ell)\approx\ell^{\tau}$ for $\ell$ a power of $m$, and a call costs about $V\ell^{\tau}$. The approximation hides the constant $\gamma(1)$ and the linear work summed over levels.

The logarithm needs $m>1$. Then $\log_m$ is increasing with $\log_m m=1$, so $s/W<m$ gives $\tau<1$. If $s/W=m$, then $\tau=1$ and no saving remains. <cite data-source="paper">This strict inequality is the source of the power saving.</cite>

As a toy example, not the paper's values, take $W=4$, $s=6$ and $m=2$. Then $s<Wm$ because $6<8$, and each doubling of the parameter multiplies $\gamma$ by $s/W=1.5$. So $\tau=\log_2 1.5\approx 0.585$. With $s=8$, $s/W=m=2$, $\tau=1$, and the saving disappears.

The argument has two steps: $s<Wm$ from the count of smaller calls, and then $s/W<m$ from dividing by $W$. The second step gives the power saving.
