The Chinese-remainder map relabels the positions of a [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md). It places the coefficients of a [radix-digit convolution](radix-digit-convolution.md) on distinct prime cyclic axes. It is the first step of the reduction in [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md), where <cite data-source="paper">the radix-digit coefficient list is placed on a product of distinct</cite> prime cyclic axes by this map.

The reason for the relabelling is as follows. A cyclic convolution of length $n$ is normally computed with transforms of length $n$. After the relabelling, the coefficients form an array with one axis per length, so a transform of length $n_\kappa$ can act along axis $\kappa$ alone and each pass uses the shorter length. The relabelling must also lose nothing, so no two positions may share an address. <cite data-source="paper">The transform lengths needed for the integer product are pairwise coprime</cite>, and the argument below shows that this is what makes the relabelling a bijection. The map is the algebraic split that Agarwal and Cooley used: <cite data-source="paper">Cooley used the Chinese remainder theorem to convert one cyclic convolution into multidimensional</cite> cyclic convolution.

Let $d$ be the number of axes and let $n_\kappa$ be the length of axis $\kappa$, for $1\le\kappa\le d$. The lengths are pairwise [coprime](coprime.md), so any two have greatest common divisor $1$, and $n=n_1\cdots n_d$. An axis is one coordinate direction of an array indexed by several coordinates. The positions of the original convolution are $k$ with $0\le k<n$.

The target is the ring $\mathbb{C}[\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_d]/(\nu_1^{n_1}-1,\ldots,\nu_d^{n_d}-1)$, where $\nu_\kappa$ is the axis variable of axis $\kappa$. A monomial $\nu_1^{\eta_1}\cdots\nu_d^{\eta_d}$ is a product of powers of the variables, where $\eta_\kappa$, an integer from $0$ to $n_\kappa-1$, is the exponent of $\nu_\kappa$. The monomials with every $\eta_\kappa$ in that range form the standard basis of the ring: there are $n$ of them, and every monomial equals one of them once each exponent is reduced modulo $n_\kappa$. The letters $\pi$, $\gamma$ and $\eta$ change meaning from earlier pages here.

The place value of axis $\kappa$ is $\pi_\kappa=n_1\cdots n_{\kappa-1}$, the number of distinct digit strings on the axes before $\kappa$, with $\pi_1=1$. The map labels each position by its [mixed-radix digits](mixed-radix-digits.md) for the axis lengths. <cite data-source="paper">Every ordinary coefficient index has a unique mixed-radix expression</cite>, so the digit of $k$ at place $\kappa$ is written $k_\kappa$, an integer from $0$ to $n_\kappa-1$, and the expansion is
$$k=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{d}k_\kappa\pi_\kappa.$$

For each axis, let $\mu_\kappa$ be an integer with $\mu_\kappa\pi_\kappa\equiv 1\pmod{n_\kappa}$. It exists because $\pi_\kappa$ is a product of lengths $n_j$ with $j<\kappa$, each coprime to $n_\kappa$, so $\pi_\kappa$ is coprime to $n_\kappa$ and is invertible modulo $n_\kappa$. The map $\Phi$ sends the formal variable $w$ of $\mathbb{C}[w]/(w^n-1)$ to
$$\Phi(w)=\prod_{\kappa=1}^{d}\nu_\kappa^{\mu_\kappa}.$$
This is well defined, because $\Phi(w)^n=\prod_\kappa\nu_\kappa^{\mu_\kappa n}=1$, each $n_\kappa$ dividing $n$. The map is extended to polynomials as a [ring homomorphism](ring-homomorphism.md), a map that preserves sums and products.

The power $\Phi(w)^k$ is $\prod_\kappa\nu_\kappa^{\mu_\kappa k}$. Let $\gamma_\kappa$ be the coordinate of position $k$ on axis $\kappa$, the exponent $\mu_\kappa k$ reduced modulo $n_\kappa$, so that $\Phi(w)^k=\prod_\kappa\nu_\kappa^{\gamma_\kappa}$ is a standard basis monomial. Substituting the digits of $k$ gives
$$\mu_\kappa k=\mu_\kappa k_\kappa\pi_\kappa+\mu_\kappa\sum_{j<\kappa}k_j\pi_j+\mu_\kappa\sum_{j>\kappa}k_j\pi_j.$$
For $j>\kappa$, $\pi_j$ contains the factor $n_\kappa$, so the last sum vanishes modulo $n_\kappa$, and $\mu_\kappa\pi_\kappa\equiv1$. Hence
$$\gamma_\kappa\equiv k_\kappa+\mu_\kappa\sum_{j<\kappa}k_j\pi_j\pmod{n_\kappa}.$$
Each coordinate is the digit on its own axis, shifted by a multiple of the lower digits.

The map is a bijection. Since $\nu_\kappa^{\mu_\kappa m}=1$ exactly when $n_\kappa\mid\mu_\kappa m$, and $\mu_\kappa$ is invertible modulo $n_\kappa$, we get $\Phi(w)^m=1$ exactly when $n_\kappa\mid m$ for every $\kappa$. The lengths are pairwise coprime, so this holds exactly when $n\mid m$, and $\Phi(w)$ has order $n$. Hence the powers $\Phi(w)^k$ with $0\le k<n$ are distinct: $\Phi(w)^k=\Phi(w)^{k'}$ with $k>k'$ would give $\Phi(w)^{k-k'}=1$ with $0<k-k'<n$. These $n$ distinct powers are standard basis monomials, so they are the whole standard basis, each hit once. <cite data-source="paper">Hence Φ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic</cite> convolution: a bijective ring homomorphism carries the product of two polynomials in $w$, which is a cyclic convolution of their coefficient lists, to the product of their images, a $d$-dimensional cyclic convolution of arrays.

Coprimality is not a formality. If two lengths were both $2$, then $\pi_2=2$ would have no inverse modulo $n_2=2$, so $\mu_2$ could not exist and $\Phi$ could not be defined this way.

Take $d=2$, $n_1=3$, $n_2=5$, so $n=15$. Then $\pi_2=3$, $\mu_1=1$ and $\mu_2=2$, since $3\cdot 2=6\equiv 1\pmod 5$. So $\Phi(w)=\nu_1\nu_2^2$. Position $k=7$ has digits $k_1=1$ and $k_2=2$, because $7=1+2\cdot 3$. The formula gives $\gamma_1=1$ and $\gamma_2\equiv 2+2\cdot 1\cdot 1=4\pmod 5$. Directly, $\Phi(w)^7=\nu_1^{7}\nu_2^{14}=\nu_1\nu_2^{4}$, which agrees. So position $7$ is stored at address $(1,4)$.
