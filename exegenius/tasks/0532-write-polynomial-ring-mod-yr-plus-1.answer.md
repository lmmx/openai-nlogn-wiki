Here $y$ is the polynomial variable, an indeterminate (a symbol with no numeric value) whose powers act as roots of unity. Here $r$ is the record length, the number of complex coefficients in a polynomial record, which is a power of two.

The polynomial ring modulo $y^r+1$ is the set of complex polynomials in which $y^r$ is identified with $-1$, so that wrap-around flips a sign. One coordinate of the array is stored in this ring, and the [convolution over the polynomial ring](polynomial-ring-convolution.md) is built on that storage, which makes the transforms of the [main result](main-theorem.md) cheap. Write $R_r=\mathbb{C}[y]/(y^r+1)$ for this set, in which two polynomials are identified when they differ by a multiple of $y^r+1$.

In the argument, one coordinate of the array is stored <cite data-source="paper">as the coefficient list of a polynomial modulo y r + 1, with r a power of two</cite>.

Every polynomial in $y$ denotes an element of $R_r$ and has a representative of degree less than $r$, where the degree is the largest exponent with a nonzero coefficient. To reduce a polynomial, replace each power $y^\sigma$ with $\sigma\ge r$, where $\sigma$ is an integer exponent of $y$, by $-y^{\sigma-r}$. This is valid because $y^\sigma=y^{\sigma-r}\,y^r=-y^{\sigma-r}$. Repeating the replacement ends at degree less than $r$.

Each element of $R_r$ has exactly one such representative. Write it as $\sum_{d=0}^{r-1}c_d\,y^d$, where $d$ is the position of a coefficient, running from $0$ to $r-1$, and $c_d$ is the complex coefficient of $y^d$. The list $(c_0,\dots,c_{r-1})$ is the coefficient list of the element. <cite data-source="paper">We always use representatives of degree less than r.</cite> Uniqueness holds because two such representatives $\sum_d c_d y^d$ and $\sum_d c'_d y^d$ that denote the same element differ by a multiple of $y^r+1$. Their difference equals $\xi\,(y^r+1)$ for a polynomial $\xi$, the quotient. Their difference has degree less than $r$, but if $\xi\neq 0$ the product has degree $\deg\xi+r\ge r$, because $y^r+1$ has leading coefficient $1$. So $\xi=0$, and the two representatives have the same coefficients.

Addition of representatives is coefficientwise and keeps degree less than $r$. For multiplication, multiply two representatives as ordinary polynomials and reduce the product as above. This does not depend on the representatives chosen: if two representatives of $g_1$ differ by $\xi\,(y^r+1)$, then after multiplying by a representative of $g_2$ they differ by that representative times $\xi\,(y^r+1)$, which is again a multiple of $y^r+1$, so both reduce to the same element. Multiples of $y^r+1$ are closed under addition and under multiplication by any polynomial, so the ring axioms that $\mathbb{C}[y]$ satisfies carry over to these classes. Hence $R_r$ satisfies the axioms of [Ring](ring.md).

Call $\zeta\in R_r$ a root of unity if $\zeta^\sigma=1$ for a positive integer $\sigma$; its order is the least such $\sigma$. Since $y^r=-1$, we have $y^{2r}=(-1)^2=1$. No smaller positive power of $y$ equals $1$. For $0<\sigma<r$, $y^\sigma$ has degree $\sigma\ge 1$, while $1$ has degree $0$, so they differ. For $r\le\sigma<2r$, $y^\sigma=-y^{\sigma-r}$ has degree $\sigma-r$, and it is not $1$: it is $-1$ when $\sigma=r$, and otherwise has degree at least $1$. So $y$ is a root of unity of order $2r$.

Take $r=4$, so $y^4=-1$, and let $g=1+2y$, with coefficient list $(1,2,0,0)$. Multiplying by $y^3$ gives $y^3+2y^4=y^3-2$, with coefficient list $(-2,0,0,1)$. The coefficient $1$ at position $0$ moved to position $3$. The coefficient $2$ at position $1$ would move to position $4$, which wraps to position $0$ with its sign reversed, giving $-2$ there.

Let $s$ be a shift amount with $0\le s<r$. Multiplying $\sum_d c_d y^d$ by $y^s$ gives $\sum_d c_d y^{d+s}$. If $d+s<r$, the coefficient $c_d$ stays unchanged and moves to position $d+s$. If $d+s\ge r$, then $y^{d+s}=-y^{d+s-r}$, so $c_d$ moves to position $d+s-r$ with its sign reversed. Multiplication by a power of $y$ therefore permutes the coefficient list and <cite data-source="paper">changes some signs</cite>. The reversal at the wrap-around is why the ring is called negacyclic: in a [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md), a coefficient that passes the end keeps its sign.

Why this helps: a fast transform of length $N$ runs in $\log_2 N$ levels, and each level multiplies entries by roots of unity. With a general complex root, each such multiplication is a complex multiplication. Here the roots are powers of $y$, which <cite data-source="paper">serve as roots of unity in the remaining axes</cite>, and multiplication by such a root <cite data-source="paper">is a signed shift of the</cite> coefficient list. A signed shift does no complex arithmetic. Each coefficient is copied to a new position, and a sign reversal changes only the sign carried with the copied value. So multiplying by one of these roots costs only data movement.

This ring gives the arithmetic for [Synthetic polynomial transforms](synthetic-transforms.md), whose roots of unity are powers of $y$.
