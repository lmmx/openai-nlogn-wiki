Radix-digit convolution is a reduction of [Exact integer multiplication](exact-integer-multiplication.md) to a convolution of short integer coefficients, obtained by writing each input as a polynomial in a power of two whose coefficients are its digits. It is the first step of the argument for [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md): the [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) and [carry propagation](carry-propagation.md) pages work on the coefficients it produces.

The paper states the reduction in one sentence: <cite data-source="paper">Splitting the inputs into radix digits reduces multiplication to convolution of short integer coefficients.</cite>

**Digits.** Let $x$ and $y$ be the two binary inputs, each of $n$ bits, with $n \ge 2$. Let $\varpi$ be the digit width, the number of bits in each digit, and let $2^\varpi$ be the radix, the base of the digits. The paper sets <cite data-source="paper">b = ⌈log2 n⌉</cite>, which is our $\varpi$; we use another letter because $b$ is the base on [Integer](integer.md). Since $\varpi \ge \log_2 n$, we have $2^\varpi \ge n$. Cut each input into blocks of $\varpi$ bits, counting from the least significant end, and pad the last block with leading zeros. Each block is a digit with a value from $0$ to $2^\varpi-1$. Let $\Upsilon = \lceil n/\varpi\rceil$ be the digit count, the number of digits in each input. Let $\mathrm{dig}_i(x)$ be the digit of $x$ at position $i$, for $0 \le i \le \Upsilon-1$, where positions are counted from 0 at the least significant end, so that $x = \sum_{i=0}^{\Upsilon-1} \mathrm{dig}_i(x)\,(2^\varpi)^i$. The digits $\mathrm{dig}_j(y)$ of $y$ are defined the same way, with $j$ the position of a digit counted from 0; this changes $j$ from its meaning on [Integer](integer.md).

**Polynomials.** Let $\vartheta$ be a formal variable, a symbol that is not a number. Let $\hat{x}(\vartheta) = \sum_{i=0}^{\Upsilon-1} \mathrm{dig}_i(x)\,\vartheta^i$ and $\hat{y}(\vartheta) = \sum_{j=0}^{\Upsilon-1} \mathrm{dig}_j(y)\,\vartheta^j$. Substituting $\vartheta = 2^\varpi$ gives back the inputs, so $\hat{x}(2^\varpi) = x$ and $\hat{y}(2^\varpi) = y$. Let $\mathrm{cv}_\varsigma$ be the coefficient of $\vartheta^\varsigma$ in the product $\hat{x}(\vartheta)\hat{y}(\vartheta)$, where the coefficient index $\varsigma$ runs over $0 \le \varsigma \le 2\Upsilon-2$. Collecting terms gives
$$\mathrm{cv}_\varsigma = \sum_{i+j=\varsigma} \mathrm{dig}_i(x)\,\mathrm{dig}_j(y),$$
with $i$ and $j$ running over $0 \le i, j \le \Upsilon-1$. This is the convolution of the digit sequences, and $\mathrm{cv}_\varsigma$ is its entry at $\varsigma$. Substituting $\vartheta = 2^\varpi$ into the product gives $xy = \sum_{\varsigma} \mathrm{cv}_\varsigma\,(2^\varpi)^\varsigma$, so the product is recovered once the $\mathrm{cv}_\varsigma$ are known. They are not binary digits yet, since a coefficient can exceed $2^\varpi-1$. [Carry propagation](carry-propagation.md) converts them into a binary string.

**Example.** Take $n=4$. Then $\varpi=2$ and $\Upsilon=2$. Let $x=1101$ (value 13) and $y=1011$ (value 11). Splitting into base-4 digits from the least significant end gives $\mathrm{dig}_0(x)=1$, $\mathrm{dig}_1(x)=3$, $\mathrm{dig}_0(y)=3$ and $\mathrm{dig}_1(y)=2$. The coefficients are $\mathrm{cv}_0=1\cdot 3=3$, $\mathrm{cv}_1=1\cdot 2+3\cdot 3=11$ and $\mathrm{cv}_2=3\cdot 2=6$. Check: $3+11\cdot 4+6\cdot 16=143=13\cdot 11$. Here $\mathrm{cv}_1=11$ is larger than a digit, so the sequence is not yet the binary product.

**Coefficient size.** Each $\mathrm{cv}_\varsigma$ is a sum of at most $\Upsilon$ products $\mathrm{dig}_i(x)\,\mathrm{dig}_j(y)$, and each product is at most $(2^\varpi-1)^2$, so $0 \le \mathrm{cv}_\varsigma \le \Upsilon(2^\varpi-1)^2$. <cite data-source="paper">Every product coefficient obeys</cite> this bound, where the paper calls the digit count $q$. Since $\Upsilon \le n \le 2^\varpi$, we get $\mathrm{cv}_\varsigma < 2^\varpi\cdot 2^{2\varpi} = 2^{3\varpi}$. Each coefficient therefore fits in $3\varpi$ bits. Since $\varpi < \log_2 n + 1$, that is $O(\log n)$ bits.

**Why the split lowers cost.** The method shows its claim in two parts: $\hat{x}(2^\varpi)\hat{y}(2^\varpi)=xy$ makes the result exact, and the coefficient bound makes the coefficients short. Short coefficients keep each convolution cheap, so the cost comes from the transforms rather than from the digits. Radix-digit convolution states its bounds in [Big-Theta notation](big-theta-notation.md), so the costs below are $\Theta$ bounds. Without the split, the digits are single bits, so there are $2n-1$ coefficients of about $\log_2 n$ bits each, and the stored volume is $\Theta(n\log n)$ bits. With the split, there are $2\Upsilon-1$ coefficients of $3\varpi$ bits. That volume is $(2\Upsilon-1)\cdot 3\varpi$ bits, and since $n/\varpi \le \Upsilon \le n/\varpi+1$, it lies between $6n-3\varpi$ and $6n+3\varpi$ bits. Because $\varpi=O(\log n)$, this is $\Theta(n)$. The split reduces the storage of the digits to a linear amount.

**Where the cost goes.** The convolution is computed with the [Discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) at a power-of-two length $\mathcal{T}$ of at least $2\Upsilon-1$. The smallest such length is below $2(2\Upsilon-1)$, so $\mathcal{T} < 4\Upsilon$. No wraparound occurs at this length, because the product has $2\Upsilon-1$ coefficients and all of them fit in $\mathcal{T}$ positions. Radix-digit convolution computes its transforms from [Butterfly](butterfly.md) operations: each level applies a butterfly to each pair of positions whose indices differ in one bit, so a transform of length $\mathcal{T}$ has $\log_2 \mathcal{T}$ levels. Since $\Upsilon = \Theta(n/\varpi)$ and $\varpi = \Theta(\log n)$, there are $\Theta(\log n)$ levels. A level has $\mathcal{T}/2 = O(\Upsilon)$ butterflies, each on coefficients of $O(\log n)$ bits, so it costs $O(n)$ bit steps. The $\Theta(\log n)$ levels therefore cost $\Theta(n\log n)$ in total. The paper makes the same count: <cite data-source="paper">Θ(log n) transform levels already costs Θ(n log n).</cite> The split removes the cost of the digits, and what remains is the cost of the transforms.
