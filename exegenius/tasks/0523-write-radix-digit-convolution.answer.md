Radix-digit convolution is a reduction of [Exact integer multiplication](exact-integer-multiplication.md) that writes each input as a polynomial in a power of two whose coefficients are its digits, so that the product becomes a convolution of short integer coefficients. It is the first step of the argument for [Integer multiplication below n log n](main-theorem.md): the [Chinese-remainder map](chinese-remainder-map.md) and [carry propagation](carry-propagation.md) pages work on the coefficients it produces.

The paper states the reduction in one sentence: <cite data-source="paper">Splitting the inputs into radix digits reduces multiplication to convolution of short integer coefficients.</cite>

**Digits.** Let $x$ and $y$ be the two binary inputs, each of $n$ bits, with $n \ge 2$. Let $b$ be the digit width, the number of bits in each digit (the letter $b$ changes meaning here). The paper sets <cite data-source="paper">b = ⌈log2 n⌉</cite>. Since $b \ge \log_2 n$, we have $2^b \ge n$. Cut each input into blocks of $b$ bits, counting from the least significant end, and pad the last block with leading zeros. Each block is a digit with a value from $0$ to $2^b-1$, and $2^b$ is the radix. Let $J = \lceil n/b\rceil$ be the number of digits in each input. Let $x_i$ be the digit of $x$ at position $i$, for $0 \le i \le J-1$, so that $x = \sum_{i=0}^{J-1} x_i (2^b)^i$. The digits $y_j$ of $y$ are defined the same way.

**Polynomials.** Let $\xi$ be a formal variable, a symbol that is not a number. Let $\hat{x}(\xi) = \sum_{i=0}^{J-1} x_i \xi^i$ and $\hat{y}(\xi) = \sum_{j=0}^{J-1} y_j \xi^j$. Substituting $\xi = 2^b$ gives back the inputs: $\hat{x}(2^b) = x$ and $\hat{y}(2^b) = y$. Let $c_m$ be the coefficient of $\xi^m$ in the product $\hat{x}(\xi)\hat{y}(\xi)$, for $0 \le m \le 2J-2$. Collecting terms gives
$$c_m = \sum_{i+j=m} x_i\, y_j,$$
with $i$ and $j$ running over $0 \le i, j \le J-1$. This is the convolution of the digit sequences $(x_i)$ and $(y_j)$, and $c_m$ is its entry $m$. Substituting $\xi = 2^b$ into the product gives $xy = \sum_{m} c_m (2^b)^m$, so the product is recovered once the $c_m$ are known. The $c_m$ are not binary digits yet, since a coefficient can exceed $2^b-1$. [Carry propagation](carry-propagation.md) turns them into one.

**Example.** Take $n = 4$. Then $b = 2$ and $J = 2$. Let $x = 1101$ (value 13) and $y = 1011$ (value 11). Splitting into base-4 digits from the least significant end gives $x_0 = 1$, $x_1 = 3$, $y_0 = 3$ and $y_1 = 2$. The coefficients are $c_0 = x_0 y_0 = 3$, $c_1 = x_0 y_1 + x_1 y_0 = 2 + 9 = 11$ and $c_2 = x_1 y_1 = 6$. Check: $3 + 11 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 16 = 143 = 13 \cdot 11$. Here $c_1 = 11$ is larger than a digit, so the sequence is not yet the binary product.

**Coefficient size.** Each $c_m$ is a sum of at most $J$ products $x_i y_j$, and each product is at most $(2^b-1)^2$, so $0 \le c_m \le J(2^b-1)^2$. <cite data-source="paper">Every product coefficient obeys</cite> this bound. Since $J \le n \le 2^b$, we get $c_m < 2^b \cdot 2^{2b} = 2^{3b}$. Each coefficient therefore fits in $3b$ bits. Since $b < \log_2 n + 1$, that is $O(\log n)$ bits.

**Why the split lowers cost.** Without the split, the digits are single bits ($b = 1$). There are then $2n-1$ coefficients, each up to $n$, so each needs about $\log_2 n$ bits. Without the split, its stored volume is written in [Big-Theta notation](big-theta-notation.md) as $\Theta(n\log n)$ bits, where $\Theta(f)$ denotes a quantity that stays between two constant multiples of $f$ for large $n$. With the split, $b = \lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ and there are $2J-1$ coefficients of $3b$ bits. That volume is $(2J-1)\cdot 3b$ bits, and since $n/b \le J \le n/b+1$ it lies between $6n-3b$ and $6n+3b$ bits. This is $\Theta(n)$ because $b = O(\log n)$. The digits therefore cost only a linear amount of storage, which is the saving the split buys.

**Where the cost goes.** The convolution is computed with the [Discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) at a power-of-two length of at least $2J-1$. That length avoids wraparound, since the product has $2J-1$ coefficients. A transform of length $2^k$ has $k$ levels, one for each bit of the position index, so the number of levels is $\Theta(\log n)$ because $J = \Theta(n/b)$ and $b = \Theta(\log n)$. Its transforms are built from [Butterfly](butterfly.md) operations, and each transform level applies them to the whole array. A level reads the array in order and writes each entry back at its new position, so the head visits every stored bit once per level, and the time is proportional to the stored bits, $\Theta(n)$ after the split. The arithmetic is proportional too. A level performs about $J$ butterflies, each on one coefficient of $O(\log n)$ bits, so it costs $O(n)$ bit steps. Each level therefore costs $\Theta(n)$, and the $\Theta(\log n)$ levels multiply this to $\Theta(n\log n)$. The split removes the cost of the digits, and the cost that remains lies in the transforms. The paper's count is that <cite data-source="paper">Θ(log n) transform levels already costs Θ(n log n).</cite>
