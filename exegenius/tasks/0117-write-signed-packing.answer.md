Signed coefficient packing turns a product of two polynomials with integer coefficients, some of them negative, into one integer product. It is the step that lets [convolution over the polynomial ring](polynomial-ring-convolution.md) compute its packed products with a single integer multiplication. <cite data-source="paper">Signed coefficient packing is a form of Kronecker substitution</cite>: the coefficients are written as blocks of one integer, and the polynomial is evaluated at the base of that integer.

Let $f$ and $g$ each have $r$ coefficients, $f = \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} f_j y^j$ and $g = \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} g_j y^j$, with $f_j$ and $g_j$ the integer coefficients at position $j$. Signed coefficient packing is based on the [polynomial ring](polynomial-ring.md): $f$ and $g$ are integer polynomials, and their product is taken there. Let $p$ be the number of bits in the magnitude bound on a coefficient, so that $|f_j| \le 2^p$ and $|g_j| \le 2^p$ for all $j$.

Packing fixes the block base $B = 2^{4p}$ and replaces $y$ by $B$. The integer $f(B) = \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} f_j B^j$ holds $f_j$ in block $j$, and <cite data-source="paper">Every block has 4p bits</cite>. A negative $f_j$ needs no separate sign: $f(B)$ is simply a signed integer. Evaluation at $B$ respects multiplication. Write $h = fg$ for the ordinary product, a polynomial with at most $2r-1$ coefficients $h_j$. Then $f(B)\,g(B) = h(B)$, so one integer multiplication computes the whole product.

Reading the blocks back needs each coefficient of $h$ to fit in its block. Each $h_j$ is a sum of at most $r$ products $f_i g_{j-i}$, each of magnitude at most $2^{2p}$, so $|h_j| \le r\,2^{2p}$. If $r \le 2^p$, then $|h_j| \le 2^{3p} < B/2$ for every $j$. The source gives the same bound: <cite data-source="paper">Every coefficient in any of these ordinary</cite> product has magnitude below $B/2$.

Signed coefficient packing recovers its coefficients from centered residues under [congruence modulo $B$](congruence-modulo.md). Let $c$ be the unique integer in $[-B/2, B/2)$ with $f(B)\,g(B) \equiv c \pmod B$. Since $h(B) = h_0 + B\sum_{j\ge 1} h_j B^{j-1}$ and $|h_0| < B/2$, the residue $c$ equals $h_0$, the constant coefficient of $h$. Subtract $c$, divide by $B$, and repeat to read $h_1$, then $h_2$, and so on. The source states the same procedure: the bound identifies each coefficient <cite data-source="paper">by its centered residue modulo B0</cite>. Negative coefficients are recovered this way, with no extra sign bit.

As a small example, take $B = 100$ instead of $2^{4p}$. Let $f = 3 - 2y$ and $g = 1 + 4y$. Then $f(100) = -197$ and $g(100) = 401$, so $f(100)\,g(100) = -78997$. The product is $h = 3 + 10y - 8y^2$, and $h(100) = 3 + 1000 - 80000 = -78997$. The centered residue of $-78997$ modulo $100$ is $3$; subtracting it and dividing by $100$ gives $-790$. The centered residue of $-790$ is $10$; subtracting it and dividing by $100$ gives $-8$. The coefficients $3$, $10$, $-8$ are recovered, the negative one with no special treatment.

Signed coefficient packing computes its integer products with the [Established O(n log n) multiplier](established-multiplier.md), used as an ordinary subroutine. The source uses the same multiplier this way: <cite data-source="paper">To multiply the polynomial records, we use the fixed-tape integer multiplier of Harvey and van</cite>. The packing does not depend on how that multiplier works inside.
