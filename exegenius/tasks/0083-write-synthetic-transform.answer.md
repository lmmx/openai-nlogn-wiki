A synthetic polynomial transform is a discrete Fourier transform whose roots of unity are powers of $y$, so each multiplication by a root is a signed shift rather than a true multiplication. It computes normalized [Cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md) on the power-of-two axes. An ordinary Fourier transform multiplies by complex roots at every butterfly; here those multiplications become shifts.

The transform is computed over the [Polynomial ring modulo yʳ + 1](polynomial-ring.md), with $r$ a power of two, so each element is a coefficient list of length $r$. Multiplication by $y$ uses $y^r=-1$. For $r=4$, with coefficients $a,b,c,d$,
$$y\,(a+by+cy^2+dy^3) = -d + ay + by^2 + cy^3.$$

The transform is built from the powers of $y$, and $y$ is a [Root of unity](roots-of-unity.md) of order $2r$. Indeed $y^r=-1$ gives $y^{2r}=1$, and no smaller positive power equals one. Here $j$ is the exponent of a twiddle factor $y^j$, an integer from $0$ to $2r-1$. Twiddle factors are the roots by which a butterfly multiplies one of its inputs, so they are powers $y^j$. Multiplying by $y^j$ is a signed shift of the coefficient list: entries that wrap past position $r-1$ change sign. <cite data-source="paper">multiplication by such a root is a signed shift of the coefficient list</cite>.

The transform is computed by successive layers of [Simultaneous butterfly layer H₀](butterfly-layer.md). A layer applies $H_0(u,v)=((u+v)/2,(u-v)/2)$ to each pair of records whose indices differ in one selected bit, after multiplying one record of each pair by its twiddle factor. So a layer needs only signed shifts, additions, subtractions and halvings. Halving is also a shift: it moves the binary point and adds one bit to the denominator, so intermediate values stay exact Gaussian dyadic numbers. <cite data-source="paper">halving adds at most one denominator bit</cite>. Each layer therefore costs only additions, subtractions and shifts of coefficient lists.
