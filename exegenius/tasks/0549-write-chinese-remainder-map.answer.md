The Chinese-remainder map is a relabeling of coefficient positions. It is applied to a [cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md) and turns it into a multidimensional cyclic convolution whose axes have pairwise [coprime](coprime.md) lengths. It places the coefficients of a [radix-digit convolution](radix-digit-convolution.md) on distinct prime cyclic axes. In the exact multiplication algorithm, <cite data-source="paper">the radix-digit coefficient list is placed on a product of distinct</cite> prime cyclic axes. It is also called the Agarwal–Cooley map, after the authors who used the [Chinese remainder theorem](chinese-remainder-theorem.md) to split a cyclic convolution: <cite data-source="paper">Cooley used the Chinese remainder theorem to convert one cyclic convolution into multidimensional</cite> cyclic convolution. This map uses <cite data-source="paper">that algebraic split</cite>.

There are $D$ cyclic axes, and axis $i$ has $\lambda_i$ positions. The lengths are pairwise coprime: any two have greatest common divisor $1$. <cite data-source="paper">The transform lengths needed for the integer product are pairwise coprime</cite> Let $\Lambda=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_D$ be the total number of positions.

The relabeling is worth doing because a cyclic convolution of length $\Lambda$ is normally computed with transforms of length $\Lambda$. After the relabeling, a transform on the product ring is applied one axis at a time, so each pass uses the shorter length $\lambda_i$. Coprimality keeps the relabeling lossless: it makes the map a bijection, so no two coefficients share an address.

Let $x$ be an indeterminate, a formal symbol that is never evaluated, whose powers index positions. (The letter $x$ changes meaning from the first input of earlier pages.) In $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^\Lambda-1)$ the powers $1,x,\dots,x^{\Lambda-1}$ form a basis, so $x$ is the generator of the ring: every element is a combination of these powers. Multiplying here reduces exponents modulo $\Lambda$, which is exactly cyclic convolution of coefficient lists.

The target is the multidimensional ring $\mathbb{C}[x_1,\dots,x_D]/(x_1^{\lambda_1}-1,\dots,x_D^{\lambda_D}-1)$, where $x_i$ is the indeterminate of axis $i$ (the letter $x_i$ changes meaning from earlier pages). Its monomials $x_1^{\eta_1}\cdots x_D^{\eta_D}$ have $\eta_i$ as the exponent of $x_i$. Reducing each exponent modulo $\lambda_i$ leaves $\lambda_i$ choices for $\eta_i$, so the monomials with $0\le\eta_i<\lambda_i$ form the standard basis of $\Lambda$ elements.

For axis $i$, let $\pi_i=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_{i-1}$ (so $\pi_1=1$), and let $\mu_i$ be an inverse of $\pi_i$ modulo $\lambda_i$, so $\mu_i\pi_i\equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda_i}$. The inverse exists because $\pi_i$ is a product of lengths each coprime to $\lambda_i$, so $\pi_i$ is coprime to $\lambda_i$.

The map $\Phi$ is defined on the generator $x$ by $\Phi(x)=\prod_{i=1}^D x_i^{\mu_i}$. It is extended to polynomials as a [ring homomorphism](ring-homomorphism.md). This is well defined: $\Phi(x)^\Lambda=\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i\Lambda}$, and each $\lambda_i$ divides $\Lambda$, so every factor is $1$.

For an integer $\nu\ge 0$ (the exponent of $\Phi(x)$; the letter $\nu$ changes meaning from earlier pages), $\Phi(x)^\nu=\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i\nu}$ equals $1$ exactly when $\lambda_i\mid\nu$ for every $i$. Indeed, for an integer $k\ge 0$ (an exponent of $x_i$), $x_i^k=1$ exactly when $\lambda_i\mid k$, because reducing $k$ modulo $\lambda_i$ leaves one of the distinct basis monomials $x_i^0,\dots,x_i^{\lambda_i-1}$, and only $x_i^0=1$ equals $1$. Since $\mu_i$ is invertible modulo $\lambda_i$, $\lambda_i\mid\mu_i\nu$ exactly when $\lambda_i\mid\nu$. The $\lambda_i$ are pairwise coprime, so they all divide $\nu$ exactly when their product $\Lambda$ does. Thus $\Phi(x)$ has order $\Lambda$, and it is invertible.

Let $e$ be an ordinary coefficient index, $0\le e<\Lambda$ (the letter $e$ changes meaning from earlier pages). The powers $\Phi(x)^e$ are distinct: suppose $\Phi(x)^a=\Phi(x)^b$ for exponents $a$ and $b$ with $0\le a<b<\Lambda$. Multiplying by the inverse of $\Phi(x)^a$ gives $\Phi(x)^{b-a}=1$, so $\Lambda\mid b-a$, impossible since $0<b-a<\Lambda$. Each $\Phi(x)^e=\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i e}$ is a basis monomial once its exponents are reduced modulo the $\lambda_i$. These $\Lambda$ distinct monomials exhaust the basis, so the powers $\Phi(x)^0,\dots,\Phi(x)^{\Lambda-1}$ <cite data-source="paper">through all target basis monomials exactly once.</cite>

So $\Phi$ maps the basis $1,x,\dots,x^{\Lambda-1}$ bijectively onto the standard basis of the target. The source confirms this: <cite data-source="paper">Hence Φ is an isomorphism and preserves cyclic</cite> convolution. A ring isomorphism carries products to products, so the cyclic convolution becomes a multidimensional one on axes $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_D$.

Each index $e$ has [mixed-radix digits](mixed-radix-digits.md) $e_1,\dots,e_D$, where $e_j$ is the digit on axis $j$ and $0\le e_j<\lambda_j$. <cite data-source="paper">Every ordinary coefficient index has a unique mixed-radix expression</cite>, namely $e=\sum_{j=1}^D e_j\pi_j$. The Chinese-remainder map computes its coordinates from the mixed-radix digits of $e$. Let $\gamma_i\in[0,\lambda_i)$ be the coordinate of $e$ on axis $i$, meaning the exponent of $x_i$ in $\Phi(x^e)$ reduced modulo $\lambda_i$. Since $\Phi(x^e)=\prod_i x_i^{\mu_i e}$, $\gamma_i$ is $\mu_i e$ reduced modulo $\lambda_i$. Substituting the digits gives
$$\mu_i e=\mu_i e_i\pi_i+\mu_i\sum_{j<i}e_j\pi_j+\sum_{j>i}\mu_i e_j\pi_j.$$
For $j>i$, $\pi_j$ contains the factor $\lambda_i$, so the last sum vanishes modulo $\lambda_i$ (<cite data-source="paper">Indeed terms with j > i are divisible by si</cite>, where the paper writes $s_i$ for $\lambda_i$). The first term is $e_i(\mu_i\pi_i)\equiv e_i$, so
$$\gamma_i\equiv e_i+\mu_i\sum_{j<i}e_j\pi_j \pmod{\lambda_i}.$$

Take $D=2$, $\lambda_1=3$, $\lambda_2=5$, so $\Lambda=15$. Then $\pi_2=3$, $\mu_1=1$, and $\mu_2=2$ because $3\cdot 2=6\equiv 1 \pmod 5$. So $\Phi(x)=x_1x_2^2$. The index $e=7$ has digits $e_1=1$ and $e_2=2$, since $7=1+3\cdot 2$. Its coordinates are $\gamma_1=1$ and $\gamma_2\equiv 2+2\cdot 1\cdot 1=4 \pmod 5$. Directly, $\Phi(x)^7=x_1^7x_2^{14}=x_1x_2^4$, since $7\equiv 1 \pmod 3$ and $14\equiv 4 \pmod 5$. Index $7$ sits at address $(1,4)$.

Let $\omega_i$ be a primitive $\lambda_i$-th root of unity. Let $\sigma$ be the row index and $\eta$ the column index, both running from $0$ to $\lambda_i-1$, and let $A_i$ be the $\lambda_i\times\lambda_i$ matrix with entry $\omega_i^{\sigma\eta}$ in row $\sigma$ and column $\eta$. This $A_i$ is the [discrete Fourier transform](discrete-fourier-transform.md) of length $\lambda_i$. Evaluating $x_i$ at the powers of $\omega_i$ is a ring isomorphism onto $\mathbb{C}^{\lambda_i}$. On all axes together, the evaluation at the point $(\omega_1^{\sigma_1},\dots,\omega_D^{\sigma_D})$ of the monomial $x_1^{\eta_1}\cdots x_D^{\eta_D}$ gives $\prod_i\omega_i^{\sigma_i\eta_i}=\prod_i (A_i)_{\sigma_i,\eta_i}$, where $\sigma_i$ is the row index on axis $i$. So the matrix of this isomorphism onto $\mathbb{C}^{\Lambda}$ is the [tensor product](tensor-product.md) of the one-axis matrices. The symbol $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker (tensor) product of matrices, so $A_1\otimes\cdots\otimes A_D$ applies $A_i$ along axis $i$ alone.

Pairwise coprimality is what makes the powers distinct. Without it, $\Phi(x)$ would have order $\operatorname{lcm}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_D)$ instead of $\Lambda$. If two lengths share a factor $g>1$, that lcm is at most $\Lambda/g<\Lambda$, so $\Phi(x)^L=\Phi(x)^0$ for some $0<L<\Lambda$.
